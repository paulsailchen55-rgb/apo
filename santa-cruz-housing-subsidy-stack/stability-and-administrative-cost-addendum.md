# From Affordability to Stability: The Missing Time-and-Risk Variable

**Project:** The Housing Subsidy Stack — A Systems Feasibility Framework for Santa Cruz County
**Status:** PROPOSED / RESEARCH IN PROGRESS
**Date:** September 24, 2026

## 1. The new distinction

Housing policy often measures whether a unit is affordable to a defined income category. This project proposes a second question: does the system produce a durable housing outcome?

A unit can satisfy an affordability definition without necessarily producing long-term housing stability.

The analysis should therefore distinguish:

**AFFORDABILITY ≠ STABILITY**

This is not an argument against affordability programs. It is a proposal to measure an outcome that point-in-time affordability calculations may not capture.

## 2. Revenue constraint

An income-restricted unit can have a lower permitted rent than an otherwise comparable unrestricted market-rate unit. That can reduce stabilized revenue and net operating income relative to an unrestricted unit.

The effect is program-specific. AMI-restricted inclusionary units, Housing Choice Vouchers, project-based assistance, LIHTC, and other programs do not have identical rent-setting, subsidy, compliance, financing, or duration mechanisms.

Santa Cruz County's 2026 Keyser Marston Associates feasibility study models affordable rents at 60% HUD AMI for low-income units and 50% HUD AMI for very-low-income units. Its rental-development pro formas examine estimated development costs, stabilized net operating income, and the financial impacts of the inclusionary requirement. citeturn0search12

**Proposed mechanism:** restricted rent/revenue → lower potential NOI → reduced debt/equity capacity → financing gap → need for another component of the subsidy stack.

This is a research hypothesis to test with project-level pro formas, not a claim that every affordable program produces a worse owner return.

## 3. Administrative transaction cost

The subsidy stack also contains costs that do not appear as construction dollars or rent.

Depending on the program, these can include eligibility verification, income and asset documentation, waiting periods, inspections, rent review, annual recertification, notices, agency coordination, reporting, compliance monitoring, tenant search time, application time, and deadlines.

Each individual requirement may have a legitimate administrative purpose. The research question is different:

> What is the combined transaction cost of making the affordability mechanism work?

That cost can fall on both sides of the housing transaction.

**Household side:** time gathering documents, application and search time, repeated eligibility procedures, deadline risk, difficulty responding to changing circumstances, and inability to absorb deposits, moving costs, or temporary income interruptions.

**Provider side:** staff time, lease-up friction, inspection and rent-review timing, compliance obligations, uncertainty about payment or approval timing, and additional administrative risk.

Administrative capacity is therefore part of the subsidy stack even when it does not appear as a line item in the development budget.

## 4. The two-sided stability problem

There are at least two interacting risks.

**Household stability risk:** Can the household remain housed when income, employment, paperwork, transportation, health, or other circumstances change?

**Provider participation risk:** Will the owner continue to offer and operate the unit under the program when the program creates additional transaction costs, rent restrictions, or compliance requirements?

The system can lose housing stability even when the nominal subsidy amount is adequate if either side becomes unstable.

## 5. Moderate-income versus deeper affordability: a testable comparison

The project should not assume that one income group is inherently more deserving than another. Instead, compare the transaction structure required to house different income categories.

A moderate-income household may still face a severe affordability problem, but may be able to use a comparatively ordinary rental transaction. A deeper affordability program may add eligibility, verification, rent-setting, reporting, or continuing compliance requirements.

The empirical question is:

> How much additional administrative machinery is required for each affordability category, and what effect does that machinery have on successful lease-up, provider participation, and long-term occupancy?

This should be measured rather than assumed.

## 6. Time and stability

A point-in-time affordability calculation does not by itself measure whether a household remains housed over years.

Proposed stability variables:

- successful lease-up;
- duration of tenancy;
- rent/payment continuity;
- income volatility;
- ability to absorb temporary income interruption;
- administrative interruption or recertification failure;
- landlord participation and retention;
- vacancy and relocation risk;
- access to deposits and moving costs;
- long-term operating and capital-replacement needs;
- continuity of the underlying subsidy or financing.

The proposed question becomes:

> How many durable housing outcomes does the system produce, rather than how many units merely satisfy an affordability category at the point of designation?

## 7. Proposed Housing Stability Yield

For research purposes, this project proposes:

**Housing Stability Yield = Affordable Units × Successful Lease-Up × Occupancy Continuity × Program Continuity × Long-Term Financial Viability**

This is a conceptual research metric, not an established government formula.

A companion concept is:

**Administrative Efficiency = Housing Stability Yield ÷ Administrative Transaction Cost**

These measures are intended to expose costs that conventional AMI and unit-count measures may not capture. They should not be used as political rankings of households, programs, or income groups.

## 8. Affordability should include stability

The expanded system is:

**Money → Revenue → Financing → Affordability → Administration → Stability**

rather than:

**Money → AMI → Affordable Unit**

The complete housing outcome is:

- Can the household obtain the home?
- Can the provider sustain the unit?
- Can the household remain housed?
- Can the program remain financially and administratively viable?

## 9. New questions for the Board, advocates, and developers

1. What staff hours and tenant hours are associated with each affordability mechanism?
2. How long does a qualifying household typically take to reach successful lease-up?
3. What percentage of designated units remain successfully occupied after 1, 5, 10, 20, and 30 years?
4. What administrative events most commonly interrupt housing stability?
5. What conditions cause owners to participate, decline participation, or leave an affordability program?
6. How much lease-up delay or vacancy risk is associated with additional program requirements?
7. How does a temporary household income interruption affect housing continuity?
8. Which costs are monetary and which are primarily time, paperwork, uncertainty, or institutional capacity?
9. Are these administrative costs included in current feasibility models?
10. Can the same affordability objective be achieved with less administrative friction?

## 10. Integration with the subsidy-stack model

The original feasibility model can now be expanded:

**Project Capacity − Land − Construction − Financing − Fees − Standards − Affordability Burden − Administrative Cost − Risk = Residual Feasibility**

For long-run analysis, add a time dimension: present value of the long-run restricted NOI minus present value of long-run costs, compliance/transaction costs, and risk equals long-run project capacity.

These are conceptual models, not legal formulas or accounting standards.

## 11. Local evidence and current County context

Santa Cruz County currently publishes feasibility and nexus studies evaluating inclusionary requirements and the Affordable Housing Impact Fee. The County says additional outreach and Housing Advisory Commission work will precede proposed ordinance or fee changes later in 2026. citeturn0search0

The County's RHNA production page separates housing production into Very Low, Low, Moderate, and Above Moderate income categories and tracks deed-restricted and non-deed-restricted units. This provides a possible data structure for adding stability measures to the existing production framework. citeturn0search8

The Santa Cruz City Measure O rental program provides a concrete local example of an affordability program combining income restrictions, maximum rents, tenant eligibility verification, landlord concurrence, Housing Authority processing, and annual income reporting. This example is city-specific and should not be generalized to every County or HUD program. citeturn0search11

## 12. Research status

**KNOWN**

- Santa Cruz County is evaluating inclusionary feasibility and Affordable Housing Impact Fee economics.
- KMA's 2026 study models affordable rents and stabilized NOI.
- Different affordability mechanisms have different program rules.

**PROPOSED**

- Measure administrative transaction cost.
- Measure long-run housing stability.
- Add provider participation risk.
- Add time and continuity to the subsidy-stack model.
- Test Housing Stability Yield as an outcome metric.

**UNKNOWN**

- Whether administrative burden materially changes long-run production or stability in Santa Cruz County.
- Which specific administrative steps create the greatest measurable friction.
- Whether moderate-income, low-income, very-low-income, and voucher-assisted pathways differ materially in lease-up time and long-run stability.
- How much of the administrative cost is already incorporated into existing feasibility models.

## 13. Core finding

The project should no longer ask only:

> How many units are affordable?

It should also ask:

> How many households can obtain and retain stable housing through the system, and what economic and administrative capacity is required to make that outcome durable?

That is the proposed bridge between the developer's pro forma, the public subsidy stack, and the tenant's lived experience.

## Evidence discipline

This addendum distinguishes documented local program structure from proposed research hypotheses. It does not establish that any affordability category, tenant group, landlord group, or program is inherently more or less desirable. The purpose is to identify measurable system effects.
