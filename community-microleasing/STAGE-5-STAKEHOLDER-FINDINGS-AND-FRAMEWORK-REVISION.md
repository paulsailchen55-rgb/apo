# Stage 5 — Stakeholder Findings and Framework Revision

## Purpose

Stage 5 converts the information gathered during exploratory conversations into a documented findings record and a revised community-microleasing framework. It does not assume that stakeholder comments represent legal decisions, universal agreement, or automatic endorsement.

## 1. Consolidate the Record

Collect interview notes, written submissions, public comments, historical research, authority information, and pilot-location observations in a consistent findings register. Preserve original statements where possible, identify whether they are attributed or anonymous, and distinguish verified facts from opinions, memories, allegations, and unresolved claims.

## 2. Classify Findings

Each finding should be assigned one or more classifications:

- Legal or regulatory requirement requiring verification.
- Property-owner or contractual condition.
- Operational or safety concern.
- Community preference or place-based expectation.
- Accessibility or participation barrier.
- Economic or livelihood concern.
- Environmental or neighborhood impact.
- Opportunity for cooperation or shared benefit.
- Conflict, disagreement, or competing use.
- Question requiring additional evidence.

AI tools may assist with sorting and summarizing, but the source record, classification rationale, and human review status should remain visible.

## 3. Compare Perspectives Without Flattening Them

Do not treat the largest number of submissions as automatic proof of authority or consensus. Record different perspectives separately, identify who has decision-making authority, and note where a concern is widely repeated, narrowly held, disputed, or unsupported by available evidence. A stakeholder's participation should not be represented as endorsement of the entire project.

## 4. Update the Framework

Revise the microleasing framework based on documented findings. Possible revisions include:

- More precise definitions of microleasing and macroleasing.
- Clearer distinctions among zoning, permits, owner authorization, and operational conditions.
- Same-day response and communication standards.
- Accessible participation methods, including non-digital and non-phone options.
- Procedures for changing, withdrawing, or reviewing conditions.
- Privacy, data minimization, and retention rules.
- Conflict-of-interest and anti-capture safeguards.
- Independent review and escalation pathways.
- Pilot evaluation measures and public reporting requirements.

Changes should be traceable: record what changed, why it changed, which evidence informed the change, and what remains uncertain.

## 5. Identify Conflicts and Tradeoffs

Create a conflict register for issues such as noise, congestion, sanitation, safety, commercial competition, public access, cultural or historical sensitivity, private property rights, and unequal participation. For each issue, identify the affected parties, the responsible authority, possible mitigations, evidence needed, and whether the issue can be addressed through conditions, redesign, relocation, scheduling, or a formal decision process.

## 6. Evaluate Candidate Pilots

A candidate pilot should be assessed for:

- A lawful and identifiable authorization pathway.
- Clearly defined location, duration, hours, and activities.
- A responsible operator or accountable party.
- Reasonable accessibility and communication arrangements.
- Manageable and measurable impacts.
- Reversibility and a defined review date.
- Multiple ways to submit concerns or request assistance.
- Protection against retaliation, secret exclusion, and dependence on one gatekeeper.
- A public-facing summary that does not disclose unnecessary personal information.

Pilot selection is not a final conclusion about the entire county. It is a limited test of specific conditions in a particular place and time.

## 7. Produce the Stage 5 Deliverables

The expected outputs are:

1. Stakeholder findings register.
2. Updated stakeholder and geographic map.
3. Authority and responsibility matrix.
4. Verified-claims and open-questions register.
5. Conflict and tradeoff register.
6. Revised microleasing framework with change notes.
7. Candidate pilot assessment.
8. Accessibility, privacy, and accountability review.
9. Public summary written in plain language.
10. Recommendation for implementation, additional research, or redesign.

## 8. Completion Criteria

Stage 5 is complete when the project can explain what was learned, whose perspectives were included and omitted, which claims were verified, which disagreements remain unresolved, what framework changes were made, and what authority would be responsible for any next decision. Completion does not require unanimity; it requires a transparent record, honest uncertainty, and a defensible path for further review.

## 9. Relationship to the Unified Civic Input Platform

The proposed unified civic input platform may eventually support this stage by accepting submissions through web, text, telephone, mail, and in-person assistance, then organizing them into a shared decision-support database. Until such a system is designed and tested, Stage 5 should use a simple documented process that preserves original submissions, records classification decisions, provides human review, and avoids treating automated summaries as the official public record.

## Closing Principle

The purpose of Stage 5 is not to manufacture agreement. It is to turn participation into a traceable, reviewable body of knowledge that helps responsible authorities and communities make more informed, lawful, accessible, and accountable decisions.
