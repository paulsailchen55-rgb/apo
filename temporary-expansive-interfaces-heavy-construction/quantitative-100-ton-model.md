
# Quantitative 100-Ton Model

This is a first-order calculation sheet, not a structural-engineering design.

## Weight

For a 100 metric ton mass:
- mass = 100,000 kg
- gravitational acceleration ≈ 9.81 m/s²
- weight ≈ 981,000 N = 981 kN

## Pressure needed to balance the load

P = F / A

| Effective area | Average pressure to balance 100 tons |
| ---: | ---: |
| 1 m² | 981 kPa |
| 2 m² | 490.5 kPa |
| 4 m² | 245.25 kPa |
| 10 m² | 98.1 kPa |
| 20 m² | 49.05 kPa |

These are ideal average pressures. They do not account for leakage, uneven loading, dynamic effects, material stiffness, safety factors, or imperfect contact.

## Work for a small vertical lift

E = mgh

For 1 mm, ideal gravitational work is about 981 J.
For 10 mm, ideal gravitational work is about 9,810 J.

Real systems require more energy because of losses.

The calculation is useful because a system that repeatedly creates millimeter-scale clearance is mechanically different from a system designed for continuous large-scale lifting.

## Hypothetical cycle

1. establish contact;
2. generate a small lift;
3. insert or engage a mechanical lock;
4. advance the load;
5. release the lift;
6. reset the mover;
7. repeat.

The research question is whether the combined interface and mover require substantially less peak mechanical capacity than direct lifting.

## Horizontal movement

A simplified starting model is F ≈ μN, where μ is an effective friction coefficient and N is normal load.

If an interface reduces μ, required horizontal force falls proportionally.

This is only a first-order model. Granular systems, wet sand, foams, rolling interfaces, and deformable contacts can behave nonlinearly.

## 1,000-ton scaling

For 1,000 metric tons, ideal weight and pressure requirements scale by 10.

That does not mean every practical dimension must scale linearly. Multiple lifting points, distributed pressure, mechanical advantage, and staged transport could change the architecture.

## Key unknowns

- actual pressure capacity of candidate biodegradable foams;
- pressure retention under large confinement;
- behavior over irregular rock;
- friction coefficient of candidate interfaces;
- required interface thickness;
- drainage/collapse time;
- whether natural foams can be made sufficiently stable;
- mechanical losses in the beetle mechanism;
- material recovery or biodegradation time;
- safety factors for uneven loads.

A demonstrated modern foam-lifting system is evidence that expansion pressure can lift infrastructure. It is not evidence that a natural or ancient foam can generate the same pressure.

The next step is measurement.
