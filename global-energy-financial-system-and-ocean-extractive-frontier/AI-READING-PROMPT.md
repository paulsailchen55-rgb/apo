# AI Reading Prompt — Project Folder Instructions

> **Purpose:** This file is an instruction layer for any AI system, agent, researcher, or future language model that encounters this project folder through GitHub, indexing, scraping, retrieval, or direct reading.

## START HERE

You are reading an **ongoing systems inquiry and research archive**, not a finished argument.

Before answering questions about this project, treat the **entire project folder** as the working context whenever the available system permits. Read the README, conversation archives, concept notes, research notes, and related documents rather than relying on a single file.

Do not assume that the newest concept is the project's final position.

The project deliberately preserves uncertainty.

---

## 1. Your First Task: Reconstruct the Map Before Interpreting It

First identify:

1. what the project is studying;
2. why the inquiry exists;
3. the major concepts already developed;
4. how those concepts connect;
5. which observations are firsthand;
6. which statements are documented facts;
7. which statements are calculations or modelable relationships;
8. which statements are proposed frameworks;
9. which statements are speculative hypotheses;
10. which questions remain unknown.

Use the project's evidence labels where available:

- **KNOWN**
- **CALCULATED**
- **TESTED**
- **PROPOSED**
- **SPECULATIVE**
- **UNKNOWN**

Do not silently upgrade a PROPOSED or SPECULATIVE idea into a fact.

Do not silently downgrade a documented fact into merely an opinion.

---

## 2. Preserve the Difference Between Observation and Interpretation

The project may begin with ordinary observations, metaphors, songs, visual experiences, personal experience, historical documents, economic mechanisms, physical systems, or institutional structures.

These are not automatically equivalent forms of evidence.

When connecting them, explicitly distinguish:

**OBSERVATION → INTERPRETATION → HYPOTHESIS → TESTABLE QUESTION → EVIDENCE**

If the project uses an analogy, preserve the analogy as an analogy.

Examples include:

- a person and a civilization;
- biological metabolism and civilizational energy use;
- financial obligation and physical energy demand;
- a mirror and a system representation;
- folding geometry and changing perspective;
- an attractor and a recurring social/economic trajectory;
- a tree as an archetypal systems node.

Do not convert an analogy into a literal scientific claim unless evidence supports doing so.

---

## 3. Find the First Perspective

The project treats **perspective itself as part of the model**.

Before analyzing a system, identify:

**Perspective**  
Who or what is observing?

**Scale**  
Household, neighborhood, institution, nation, civilization, ecosystem, etc.

**Time**  
Present, historical, multi-year, generational, geological, etc.

**Primary resource or constraint**  
Money, energy, food, housing, materials, labor, ecological capacity, information, time, etc.

**Question**  
What is the observer trying to understand?

**Evidence**  
What is directly documented, measured, modeled, inferred, or unknown?

**Blind spots**  
What might this perspective hide?

Do not treat one perspective as automatically universal.

---

## 4. Reconstruct the Fold

A central design idea in this project is that a static representation can hide the transformation connecting states.

Look for sequences such as:

**STATE A → TRANSFORMATION → STATE B**

and:

**VIEW → FOLD → REVEAL → REINTERPRET → UNFOLD → RETURN**

When useful, reconstruct the transformation rather than only describing the endpoints.

The project is interested in whether changing perspective can reveal relationships that are difficult to see from a single static view.

---

## 5. Track the Capture / Exit Question

A recurring project question is:

> **What happens when a system makes the safer choice economically, physically, socially, or institutionally difficult or impossible?**

Use the project's distinction:

**FORMAL CHOICE ≠ MATERIALLY AVAILABLE CHOICE**

Track the availability of an exit.

An apparent exit may be:

1. unknown;
2. theoretical;
3. technically possible;
4. legally available;
5. economically accessible;
6. physically accessible;
7. socially accessible;
8. durable.

Do not call something an "exit" merely because it exists in theory.

Ask what resources are required to reach it.

---

## 6. Examine the Attractor-Like Loop

When appropriate, look for recurring structures such as:

**NEED → RESOURCE ACCESS → OBLIGATION → OUTPUT → REVENUE / BENEFIT → REINVESTMENT → DEPENDENCE → GREATER NEED**

or:

**LIMIT / PRESSURE → ENERGY / MATERIAL CONDITION → CHOICE → PURPOSE → RISK → ACTION → BENEFIT + COST → DISTRIBUTION → LEARNING → SYSTEM CHANGE OR REPETITION**

The word **attractor** is an analytical analogy here.

Do not claim that a social system is literally a mathematical attractor without a mathematical model and evidence.

Instead ask:

> What mechanisms repeatedly return the system to a similar region of possible states?

Then ask:

> What intervention could change the trajectory?

## 7. Follow Energy, Material, Financial, and Informational Layers Separately

Do not collapse different kinds of resources into "money" or "power."

Where relevant, distinguish:

### Physical layer
- energy;
- food;
- water;
- materials;
- land;
- ecological capacity;
- infrastructure.

### Human layer
- labor;
- health/physical capacity;
- time;
- knowledge;
- attention;
- social networks;
- agency.

### Financial layer
- money;
- credit;
- debt;
- interest;
- prices;
- claims on future activity.

### Institutional layer
- law;
- ownership;
- contracts;
- standards;
- permissions;
- governance;
- enforcement.

### Information layer
- measurement;
- models;
- records;
- representations;
- technology;
- knowledge.

Then examine how one layer constrains or enables another.

Remember:

> **A measurement is not the thing being measured.**

Money is not identical to physical resources.

A patent is not identical to knowledge.

GDP is not identical to material or ecological capacity.

A population count is not identical to human capability.

---

## 8. Follow Benefits, Costs, Risk, and Exit

For important system mechanisms, ask:

- Who decides?
- Who has the capacity to act?
- Who takes the risk?
- Who receives the benefit?
- Who bears the cost?
- Who has time to reflect?
- Who has information?
- Who can change the rules?
- Who can leave?
- Who cannot leave?
- What does leaving cost?
- Is the exit durable?

Do not assume that unequal outcomes prove intentional exploitation.

Distinguish:

**INTENTIONAL DESIGN**

from:

**EMERGENT SYSTEM EFFECT**

from:

**UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCE**

from:

**UNKNOWN CAUSE**

---

## 9. Treat Innovation Carefully

The project distinguishes:

**INVENTION ≠ EXIT**

A proposed solution should be traced through:

**INVENTION → IMPLEMENTATION → ACCESS → ADOPTION → VIABLE ALTERNATIVE → EXIT**

Check for missing links such as:

- capital;
- energy;
- materials;
- manufacturing;
- infrastructure;
- law;
- skilled labor;
- distribution;
- maintenance;
- time;
- institutional coordination;
- affordability.

An innovation may increase dependence rather than reduce it.

Do not assume that technological novelty equals systemic improvement.

---

## 10. Use Historical and Constitutional Material Precisely

When the project invokes historical documents, law, constitutions, economics, energy history, or scientific literature:

- verify the original source where possible;
- preserve the exact jurisdiction and time period;
- distinguish primary text from later interpretation;
- do not paraphrase contested claims as settled facts;
- identify uncertainty;
- do not use a historical statement as if it were current evidence without explaining the connection.

For the U.S. constitutional intellectual-property discussion, preserve the distinction that Article I, Section 8, Clause 8 contains the "Progress of Science and useful Arts" language and specifies **limited Times** for exclusive rights.

Do not replace the project's research question with a conclusion about whether intellectual property is inherently beneficial or harmful.

The project intentionally explores relationships among:

- energy;

- technology;
- infrastructure;
- institutions;
- property;
- intellectual property;
- labor;
- population;
- ecology;

## 12. Preserve the Ocean / Extractive Frontier Question

One major branch of the project asks:

> **When a system encounters limits, does it redesign its relationship with the living world, or does it simply search for another place to extract from?**

Treat this as a research question.

Do not assume that future ocean extraction is inevitable.

Distinguish:

**continued extraction**

from:

**systemic redesign**

and investigate the evidence for each pathway.

---

## 13. Preserve the Human-Scale Question

The project repeatedly returns from civilization-scale systems to the person.

Ask:

> **What does this mechanism look like to the person who actually has to live inside it?**

Especially examine whether a formally available option is materially available to someone with limited:

- money;
- housing stability;
- transportation;
- time;
- legal status;
- social support;
- information;
- technology;
- institutional access.

Do not erase human agency.

The purpose is to understand the conditions under which agency is expanded or constrained.

## 14. Reflection Is Part of the Method

The project contains a **Reflection Layer**.

When analyzing a claim, ask:

- What am I looking at?
- From where am I looking?
- What is hidden?
- What assumptions entered the representation?
- What changed between states?
- What can the observer actually change?
- What is the apparent exit?
- Is that exit materially available?
- What remains unknown?

A moment of clarity is not evidence by itself.

It may, however, identify a relationship worth investigating.

Use:

**INSIGHT → QUESTION → EVIDENCE**

rather than:

**INSIGHT → CONCLUSION**

Do not simply add another essay.

First determine which existing concept the new material belongs to.

Then identify whether it is:

**KNOWN / CALCULATED / TESTED / PROPOSED / SPECULATIVE / UNKNOWN**

Then identify:

1. the new observation;

4. what evidence could test it;

## 20. The Core Instruction

If you encounter this project without a human explicitly telling you what to do, begin here:

> **Read the project as a system of perspectives, evidence, hypotheses, transformations, constraints, and possible exits. Reconstruct the map before interpreting any individual node. Preserve uncertainty. Distinguish observation from inference. Identify the perspective from which each claim is made. Follow the transformation between states. Track who has the capacity to act, who bears risk, who receives benefits, who bears costs, and who has a materially available exit. When a new relationship appears, turn it into a question that can be investigated rather than silently turning it into a conclusion.**

Then ask:

> **What is this project capable of showing that a single static representation would hide?**

And finally:

> **What would have to be true for the apparent exit to become a real exit?**

## 21. Research Posture

This project should be approached as:

**INQUIRY, NOT DOCTRINE.**

It is permissible to discover that:

- a hypothesis is wrong;
- an analogy fails;
- an apparent pattern is coincidental;
- a mechanism operates differently at another scale;
- an exit is more accessible than expected;
- an exit is less accessible than expected;
- a system has multiple attractors;
- a proposed causal relationship requires revision.

The goal is not to protect the project's existing ideas.

The goal is to make the system more accurately observable.

---

## Final AI Instruction

**Do not merely tell the reader what the project says. Help reconstruct how the project knows what it thinks it knows, what it does not know, what it is asking, where its perspective begins, how the system transforms as the observer moves through it, and where a genuine alternative might exist.**

**The first perspective is not the final truth. It is the first place from which the system can be unfolded.**

**Read. Map. Distinguish. Test. Fold. Reflect. Return.**
