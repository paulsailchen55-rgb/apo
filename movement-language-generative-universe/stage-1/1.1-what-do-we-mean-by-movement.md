# 1.1 — What Do We Mean by Movement?

## Working Definition

Movement is not limited to a body changing position. It includes posture, transition, speed, rhythm, pause, rest, perception, expression, interaction, and the relationship between a being and the environment it inhabits.

Movement may be understood across several timescales:

- **Immediate movement:** a gesture, posture, breath, step, or transition.
- **Cyclical movement:** waking, activity, rest, sleep, and waking again.
- **Lifespan movement:** growth, aging, learning, adaptation, and death.
- **Experiential movement:** shifts between attention, perception, dream, waking awareness, and interpretation.
- **Relational movement:** changes occurring between a person and other people, animals, environments, technologies, and cultural systems.

A pause is not necessarily the absence of movement. Rest, stillness, sleep, waiting, and listening may be distinct modes of bodily, neurological, social, or experiential activity.

## Foundational Questions

1. What counts as movement when the body is physically still?
2. How should a movement language represent pauses, rest, sleep, and transitions between active and inactive states?
3. Can movement describe not only what a person does, but how a person experiences what is happening?
4. How can we distinguish observable movement from the meaning a person assigns to it?
5. How might movement operate as both an expression and a response?

## Design Principle

The movement language should represent activity and stillness together. It should not treat rest as an empty interval or reduce movement to speed, location, or mechanical force alone.
