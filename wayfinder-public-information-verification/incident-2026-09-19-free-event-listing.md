# Field Observation: Advertised Free Event and Ticket Requirement

## Date

September 19, 2026

## Purpose of this record

This note documents a firsthand test of public event information. It is preserved as an observation for future research into reliable listings, last-minute changes, access barriers, and ways for people to verify information before traveling.

## Reported experience

The event was encountered through information describing a free pre-show connected with a block party show. I walked to the location intending to attend the free event. At the ticket area, staff stated that there was no free show and that tickets were required.

This created a discrepancy between the information that led me to attend and the conditions communicated at the location. It is not yet established whether the original information was inaccurate, whether the event conditions changed shortly before arrival, or whether the free pre-show and ticketed show were being distinguished differently by different parties.

## Independent corroboration

An older woman who had also seen the event information in Good Times asked whether I had seen the block party show. I explained that I had been heading there but was told that tickets were required. She said she had gone to the same event and had been told the same thing.

This is an additional firsthand report from another attendee. It does not by itself establish the cause of the discrepancy, but it suggests that the experience was not necessarily limited to one person.

## What is known and unknown

Known from personal observation:

- The event was understood to include a free pre-show.
- I traveled to the event intending to attend without paying for a ticket.
- Ticket-area personnel stated that there was no free show and that tickets were required.
- Another attendee independently reported receiving the same information.

Not yet verified:

- The exact original wording of the advertisement.
- The publisher, publication date, and last update time of the listing.
- Whether the event organizers changed the arrangement before the event.
- Whether a free portion existed at another time, entrance, or location.
- Whether the listing included restrictions that were not visible or understood.

## Questions for further research

1. What exact wording did the original advertisement use?
2. Was the word “free” attached to the entire event, a pre-show, or a specific portion?
3. Was the listing updated or corrected before the event began?
4. Did the organizers publish a last-minute change through another channel?
5. Were ticket requirements stated clearly enough for someone planning to attend for free?
6. How can people preserve screenshots, dates, and links without collecting unnecessary personal information?
7. Could event listings show a verified status and the time of their latest confirmation?
8. What process should attendees use to report a discrepancy respectfully?
9. How can independent reports be compared to identify repeated access problems?
10. What information would help a person decide whether traveling to an advertised free event is worthwhile?

## Preliminary research direction

A public-information verification system could compare an advertised claim with conditions encountered in the field. Such a system should preserve the original wording, time, source, location, and firsthand observation while allowing for ordinary explanations such as schedule changes, communication errors, separate event segments, or capacity and ticketing restrictions.

The goal is not to assign blame automatically. The goal is to make public information more dependable and to give people a way to document and investigate discrepancies when they occur.
