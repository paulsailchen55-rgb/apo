# Nonprofit Partnership, Contracting, and Risk Allocation Framework

## Status

Conceptual planning and legal research notes. This document is not legal advice and does not establish that a proposed operation is permitted. The operating nonprofit should obtain written guidance from the applicable local enforcement agencies and qualified legal/insurance professionals before launch.

## 1. Purpose

The mobile food-and-water service should be designed so that an established nonprofit, a contracted operator, or a supervised volunteer team can operate it through a documented relationship rather than relying on informal exceptions or inconsistent personal knowledge.

The goal is to create a repeatable operating package that a qualified organization can review, adopt, insure, train, inspect, and operate at approved locations.

## 2. Possible operating relationships

### A. Established nonprofit sponsor

The nonprofit is the primary operator and is responsible for:

- Obtaining and maintaining applicable registrations, permits, and approvals.
- Establishing the food, water, sanitation, safety, accessibility, and incident policies.
- Hiring or supervising operators and volunteers.
- Maintaining supplier records, ingredient records, batch records, training records, and service logs.
- Maintaining insurance and reporting incidents.
- Entering into property-use agreements with owners or managers.

This structure may be easier to evaluate when the nonprofit already has an established compliance history, trained staff, insurance, and relationships with local agencies. It does not automatically guarantee faster approval or exemption from requirements.

### B. Nonprofit employee

A nonprofit may hire an employee to perform defined duties. The job description should identify the employee's authority and limits, including:

- Startup and shutdown checks.
- Food and water verification.
- Packet selection and receipt generation.
- Customer-facing explanation of ingredients and warnings.
- Hot-water and equipment safety.
- Sanitation and handwashing procedures.
- Incident escalation and documentation.
- Prohibition on diagnosing allergies or making individualized medical claims.

The nonprofit should provide training, supervision, written procedures, and workers' compensation and employment compliance as applicable.

### C. Independent contractor

A nonprofit may seek to contract with an individual or business to provide defined services, but the label "independent contractor" does not by itself determine legal status. California worker-classification rules must be reviewed based on the actual relationship, including control, independence, business structure, and the nature of the work.

A contractor agreement should define:

- Scope of services and operating locations.
- Required permits, licenses, certifications, and training.
- Who supplies and owns the equipment.
- Who buys, stores, transports, and verifies ingredients.
- Who controls the operating procedures.
- Insurance requirements and proof of coverage.
- Reporting, records, audits, and inspection access.
- Equipment breakdown, recalls, contamination, and emergency shutdown procedures.
- Payment, scheduling, termination, and dispute procedures.
- Responsibility for subcontractors and assistants.

A contractor should not be used as a way to avoid food-safety, employment, licensing, or public-health obligations.

### D. Volunteer team

Volunteers can perform permitted duties when the nonprofit has a structured volunteer program. Volunteer status does not mean that every task is legally unrestricted. The nonprofit should determine which duties require training, certification, supervision, or approval by the local enforcement agency.

The volunteer program should include:

- Written role descriptions.
- Orientation and task-specific training.
- A designated supervisor.
- Sign-in/sign-out records.
- Illness exclusion and hygiene rules.
- Clear limits on handling open food, hot water, equipment, money, personal data, and medical questions.
- An escalation process for unsafe conditions or participant complaints.

Federal Volunteer Protection Act protections generally contain conditions and exceptions, and they do not eliminate the nonprofit's own liability or override applicable licensing and safety requirements. The organization should not promise volunteers complete immunity.

## 3. Regulatory pathway in Santa Cruz County

Santa Cruz County Environmental Health identifies different pathways depending on the operation. Limited Service Charitable Feeding Operations may cover specified activities such as distributing certain prepackaged foods, heating or assembling small volumes of commercially prepared foods or ingredients, or reheating and portioning commercially prepared foods. Activities outside the limited categories may require plan review and a health permit.

A compact mobile food preparation operation, mobile food preparation unit, or mobile support unit may require a separate plan-review process before permitting. The proposed atmospheric-water equipment, heating system, ingredient storage, dispensing arrangement, wastewater plan, handwashing facilities, and transport configuration should be submitted for consultation before purchase or construction.

The nonprofit should request a written determination of the applicable category rather than assuming that free distribution, nonprofit status, or use of volunteers eliminates the need for approval.

## 4. Core agreement stack

The project should use a coordinated set of agreements instead of one broad release form.

### 4.1 Nonprofit–manufacturer or equipment supplier agreement

Address:

- Exact model numbers and approved use cases.
- Installation, commissioning, maintenance, and service requirements.
- Warranty term, exclusions, and authorized repairs.
- Water-contact materials and sanitation requirements.
- Software, firmware, cybersecurity, and update responsibilities.
- Sensor calibration and data retention.
- Performance limitations, including water-production rate and environmental conditions.
- Recall, defect notification, and safety-alert procedures.
- Spare parts and service response times.
- Insurance and indemnity provisions, subject to legal review.
- Allocation of responsibility for misuse, unauthorized modification, poor maintenance, and ordinary wear.

The nonprofit should not represent that manufacturer certification proves the complete safety or legal suitability of the entire assembled system. Each subsystem and the complete operating configuration may require separate review.

### 4.2 Nonprofit–food supplier agreement

Address:

- Product specifications and approved ingredient list.
- Allergen declarations and cross-contact controls.
- Batch/lot numbers and expiration or best-by information.
- Storage and transportation requirements.
- Recall notification and traceability.
- Supplier change notification.
- Certificates of analysis or other verification where appropriate.
- Packaging integrity and labeling requirements.
- Responsibility for inaccurate, incomplete, or changed ingredient information.
- Delivery rejection procedures.

### 4.3 Nonprofit–operator agreement

The operator agreement should assign operational duties and require compliance with the nonprofit's standard operating procedures. It should identify who may stop service, who may approve a restart, and who must be contacted when water testing, equipment, sanitation, temperature, labeling, or ingredient records are incomplete.

### 4.4 Property-use agreement

The site agreement should cover:

- Exact location and operating hours.
- Whether the equipment may be wheeled in and removed.
- Access to restrooms, waste disposal, and emergency routes.
- Noise and music limitations.
- Power, water, and wastewater arrangements.
- Security, storage, and overnight restrictions.
- Cleanup and property damage.
- Insurance certificates and additional-insured requirements where requested.
- Cancellation, weather, crowd, or safety conditions.
- Responsibility for complaints and coordination with neighboring businesses.

### 4.5 Volunteer participation and confidentiality documents

Volunteers should receive plain-language role descriptions, safety instructions, privacy rules, and acknowledgement of training. A participant-facing food receipt or acknowledgement should not be treated as a blanket waiver of negligence, product liability, food-safety duties, or statutory rights. Any release or assumption-of-risk language must be drafted and reviewed by qualified counsel and should never replace accurate ingredient disclosure.

## 5. Liability allocation principles

The project should distinguish among:

- Product defect or inaccurate manufacturer information.
- Supplier labeling or allergen error.
- Nonprofit selection, storage, handling, supervision, or distribution.
- Operator or volunteer deviation from procedure.
- Equipment maintenance or sanitation failure.
- Participant-specific medical conditions or unknown allergies.
- Property damage, crowd issues, noise, and transportation risks.

Contracts can allocate responsibility between parties, require insurance, establish notice and cooperation duties, and define remedies. They cannot reliably erase all liability, override public-health laws, or guarantee that a participant cannot sue. Avoid using a broad "non-suing" document as the main safety strategy.

California Civil Code section 1714.25 provides certain protections for qualifying charitable food donations, subject to conditions and exceptions including gross negligence or intentional misconduct. The protection should be reviewed against the actual operation and should not be assumed to cover every prepared-food, water, equipment, or service-related claim.

## 6. Information availability as a contractual duty

The following should be mandatory operating requirements:

1. Every distributed food component has a current ingredient record.
2. Major allergens and relevant cross-contact information are available before distribution.
3. The operator can produce a printed receipt or provide a phone-accessible record.
4. The receipt identifies the exact packet or batch when feasible.
5. Ingredient records are updated when a supplier or formulation changes.
6. If required information is missing, the packet is withheld from distribution.
7. The operator does not diagnose allergies or promise that a product is safe for a particular person.
8. Water status, treatment, testing, and service-session records are available to the operator and retained according to the approved recordkeeping plan.
9. Participants can request a paper copy without owning a phone.
10. The system records corrections, recalls, complaints, and incidents.

## 7. Vetted-operator model

A scalable model could have three levels:

### Level 1: Approved sponsoring organization

The nonprofit demonstrates legal identity, insurance, governance, training, and a documented food/water safety program.

### Level 2: Approved operating team

Specific employees, contractors, and volunteers complete role-specific training and are authorized for defined tasks.

### Level 3: Approved site and session

Each location and service session is checked for property permission, applicable permits, operating hours, sanitation, emergency access, and equipment readiness.

This structure allows a repeatable service package while preserving the ability of local authorities and property owners to impose site-specific conditions.

## 8. Recommended pilot sequence

1. Select a sponsoring nonprofit with an existing compliance and insurance structure.
2. Describe the exact menu, water system, equipment, service method, and site conditions.
3. Meet with Santa Cruz County Environmental Health before acquiring or modifying equipment.
4. Obtain written clarification of the applicable charitable, mobile, temporary, water, and food-service requirements.
5. Ask the insurer to review the complete operating model, not only the nonprofit's general policy.
6. Draft supplier, manufacturer, operator, volunteer, and property agreements.
7. Conduct a controlled pilot with a limited menu and documented shutdown criteria.
8. Audit ingredient receipt accuracy, water records, sanitation, participant questions, and incident handling.
9. Revise the operating package before expanding to other nonprofits or jurisdictions.

## 9. Open legal and operational questions

- Does the exact water-from-air system qualify as a potable water source under the applicable authority's requirements?
- What treatment, laboratory testing, monitoring, and recordkeeping are required?
- Is the proposed activity a Limited Service Charitable Feeding Operation, mobile food facility, temporary food facility, or another category?
- Which tasks may volunteers perform without certification, and which require training or cards?
- What worker-classification rules apply if one individual is paid as a contractor?
- What insurance limits and endorsements will manufacturers, property owners, and nonprofits require?
- Which warranties remain valid when equipment is transported, modified, or operated outdoors?
- What data may be collected from participants, and how will privacy be protected?
- What procedure applies when an ingredient record is missing, a supplier changes a formula, or a recall occurs?
- Which responsibilities remain with the manufacturer, supplier, nonprofit, operator, and site owner after an incident?

## Working principle

The service should be easy for an established nonprofit or properly contracted operator to adopt, but it should never depend on informal tolerance, hidden responsibilities, undocumented ingredients, or a waiver used in place of safe design. The goal is a documented chain of authorization, training, information, equipment responsibility, and accountability from manufacturer to supplier to nonprofit to operator to participant.
