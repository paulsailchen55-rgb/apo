# International Policy Proposal

## Working title

Open Market Computing Products

## International premise

The concept is not inherently U.S.-specific.

Any nation could investigate whether its laws and markets preserve enough room for people to purchase computing products that they can legally and technically modify, repair, study, and develop.

## Proposed framework

A country could examine whether a parallel market category should be encouraged in which:

- proprietary commercial products remain legal and available;
- manufacturers retain legitimate intellectual-property protections;
- at least some products are deliberately designed for independent modification and development;
- owners receive meaningful legal permission to work on those products;
- necessary interfaces and documentation are available;
- safety, security, communications, and other regulated systems receive appropriate treatment.

## Comparative research

The project should examine jurisdictions with relevant policies concerning:

- right to repair;
- interoperability;
- open-source software;
- open-source hardware;
- technological protection measures;
- consumer ownership;
- competition law;
- digital markets;
- product durability;
- repair information;
- government procurement;
- educational technology.

The purpose is not to assume that one country's solution should be copied everywhere.

## Petition model

People in different countries could use the framework to ask their own governments:

> Does our country have a sufficiently open consumer-computing market for independent people and organizations to develop on hardware they can lawfully purchase?

They could then adapt the question to their own legal system, institutions, industries, and policy priorities.
