# Trash Collection and Protect-and-Preserve Reflection

- **Author:** Paul Statchen
- **Date:** 2026-09-18
- **Location:** Santa Cruz area; exact collection location not recorded
- **Status:** Personal field note and conceptual reflection; not a verified environmental assessment

## Immediate observation

While walking and picking up litter, I collected small plastic items and carried them farther down the street to place them in a public trash receptacle. The action was practical and local: remove discarded material from the walking environment and place it where it could enter an established waste-collection pathway.

The note does not claim that the collected items were weighed, categorized completely, or assessed for their ecological effects.

## Personal motivation and useful action

Trash collection can be both environmental stewardship and a way of directing difficult or unsettled emotional, mental, or physical energy toward useful action. The process may resemble **sublimation**, a psychological term for redirecting difficult impulses or feelings into socially constructive activity. This is a tentative description, not a clinical assessment.

The action is understood as being for more than one party: for the environment, for the person taking action, and potentially for other people who observe the action and become encouraged to participate. A small act may contribute to a broader collective pattern in which people notice cleaner public spaces, care for surrounding life, and develop more protective relationships with the coast and community.

## Protect and preserve as relationship

“Protect and preserve” should not be treated only as a technical label or a permanent personal status. It can also describe a relationship among people, other living beings, materials, places, and consequences over time.

A result may function as a **witness for** or a **witness against** the choices that produced it. In the user's theological framing, when an outcome reveals harm, the appropriate response may involve recognition, repentance, and changed conduct. In a secular project record, this can be translated into:

- noticing harmful results;
- acknowledging responsibility where responsibility is established;
- changing methods or behavior;
- monitoring whether the change reduces harm; and
- treating later outcomes as evidence that can support or challenge the original approach.

The archive should distinguish measurable environmental outcomes from personal spiritual interpretation. It should not claim that a person's inner state, righteousness, consciousness, or readiness to protect can be objectively ranked against another person's inner state.

## Authority and governance

For structures or technologies affecting public waters, shorelines, rivers, transportation, wildlife, or public safety, the relevant government authorities should retain formal authority to approve, monitor, pause, modify, and require removal of projects according to applicable law and public responsibilities.

Future project records should identify the actual authorities, agencies, legal powers, public participation pathways, and accountability mechanisms rather than treating “the government” as one undifferentiated institution.

## Environmental baseline and uncertainty

Environmental damage can be difficult to calculate when monitoring records do not extend far enough into the past to establish a reliable baseline. Ocean, river, and watershed conditions vary across time and location, and historical “stable state” assumptions may be incomplete or contested.

Project records should therefore identify:

- the baseline period and location;
- what was measured and how;
- what remains unknown;
- whether the measurement represents short-term conditions or long-term patterns; and
- which conclusions are established, preliminary, speculative, or interpretive.

The absence of a complete historical baseline should not be used as proof that an activity is harmless. It should be recorded as an uncertainty requiring cautious monitoring and transparent limits on conclusions.

## Equity and public access

Project design should focus especially on people who are vulnerable, sick, hungry, thirsty, persecuted, disabled, aging, or otherwise unable to function easily within existing systems. The goal is not simply to make strong or already-capable people more capable, but to raise access and practical opportunity toward a more equitable standard.

An example is improved access to the Santa Cruz jetty, including access for older people and people using wheelchairs or living with disabilities. Changes that allow more people to reach public places can be meaningful evidence of inclusive design, while the exact accessibility conditions should still be documented and verified.

Human needs can change over a lifetime. Someone who is strong or independent at one point may later need the same accommodations or technologies previously associated with people considered vulnerable. This supports designing infrastructure and services for changing needs rather than dividing people permanently into “strong” and “weak” categories.

## Local knowledge, possibilities, and evidence status

The archive should preserve more than verified claims. It should also retain possibilities, dreams, imaginative hypotheses, spiritual reflections, and early concepts that may later become research questions or practical designs.

These categories should be visibly separated, for example:

- **Observed:** directly noticed or recorded by the author;
- **Reported:** received from another person or source but not independently verified;
- **Verified:** supported by reliable documentation or repeatable evidence;
- **Research question:** a question requiring investigation;
- **Speculative concept:** a possibility not yet demonstrated;
- **Interpretive or spiritual reflection:** meaning assigned by the author or a community;
- **Proposal:** a possible action requiring legal, technical, ecological, and public review.

A possibility does not need to be presented as an established fact to deserve preservation in an archive. Its value may be that it gives people something to investigate, test, refine, reject, or transform from an imagined form into a material one.

## Future archive work

For relevant projects, identify and later research:

- responsible government agencies and authorities;
- community organizations and local knowledge holders;
- affected residents, workers, fishermen, disabled people, unhoused people, and other stakeholders;
- approval, monitoring, appeal, pause, shutdown, and removal processes; and
- who is responsible for long-term maintenance, restoration, and costs.

This agency and stakeholder mapping should be done as a separate research task, with current sources and clear distinctions between confirmed jurisdiction and possible relevance.

## Guiding reflection

Small actions can have several outcomes at once: environmental benefit, personal reorientation, public example, and collective cultural influence. The archive should record these layers without claiming that one small action proves a large ecological result. The working principle is:

> Protect and preserve through attention, useful action, accountability, accessibility, and willingness to change when the results show harm.
