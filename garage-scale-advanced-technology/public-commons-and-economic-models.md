# Public Commons and Economic Models for Advanced Technology

**Date:** 2026-09-18  
**Status:** Research and design question.

## The central tension

The project is not based on the idea that manufacturers should provide everything for free.

Advanced technology requires investment, engineering labor, manufacturing infrastructure, testing, certification, intellectual property, and risk-bearing.

The question is whether commercial success and a substantial public technical commons can coexist.

## Working preference

The user's current informal preference is for a substantial portion of the technology ecosystem to remain accessible for public experimentation, tentatively **above 50%, perhaps around 60% or more**.

This is a design preference and research hypothesis, not an established economic or policy standard.

The more useful question is:

> Which layers need to remain accessible for people to meaningfully understand, repair, modify, teach, and experiment with advanced technology?

## Possible open layers

The public layer might include some combination of:

- physical interface specifications;
- module dimensions;
- diagnostic protocols;
- reference designs;
- repair documentation;
- software source code;
- firmware source where practical;
- development tools;
- test procedures;
- educational materials;
- independent measurement tools;
- interoperability standards.

A manufacturer could still sell:

- chips;
- modules;
- complete devices;
- specialized manufacturing;
- support;
- certification;
- proprietary implementations;
- services.

The actual division should be researched rather than predetermined.

## Licensing questions

Potential models to investigate include:

- open hardware licenses;
- open-source software with commercial support;
- commercial hardware with open documentation;
- reference designs with competing manufacturing;
- dual licensing;
- public-interest standards;
- community repair agreements;
- educational licenses;
- shared manufacturing designs.

No single model is assumed to solve the problem.

## Subscription dependence

One concern is a future in which hardware remains physically present but important capabilities become dependent on continuing subscriptions or manufacturer-controlled cloud services.

Questions include:

- Which functions should continue working offline?
- Which diagnostics should remain local?
- Can users retain ownership of physical modules independent of a service?
- What happens when a company ends support?
- Can an independent system replace a discontinued service?
- Can data remain exportable?

These are architectural questions as much as economic ones.

## Technological agency

The public commons should be evaluated by what people can actually do.

Useful measures might include:

- Can a person identify a module?
- Can they test it?
- Can they replace it?
- Can they repair it?
- Can they obtain the documentation?
- Can they build compatible tools?
- Can they change software?
- Can they use the device without unnecessary service dependence?
- Can they teach someone else how it works?

## Cultural futures

Two contrasting thought experiments are useful.

One is a cyberpunk-like trajectory: advanced technology is everywhere, but ordinary people experience it as opaque infrastructure controlled by manufacturers, subscriptions, specialized service organizations, or inaccessible expertise.

The other is a more distributed, repairable, participatory trajectory: advanced technology coexists with local workshops, open documentation, replaceable modules, public experimentation, and practical understanding.

These are scenarios, not predictions.

The purpose is to examine which architectural and economic choices increase or decrease technological agency.

## Research questions

1. What percentage of an advanced technology stack needs to remain accessible for meaningful public experimentation?
2. Which layers provide the greatest leverage?
3. Can manufacturers earn sustainable returns while keeping interfaces open?
4. What licensing arrangements have worked in practice?
5. What happens to repairability when cloud services are discontinued?
6. How should offline functionality be preserved?
7. How can users retain data and device functionality after vendor support ends?
8. Which public standards create real interoperability rather than nominal openness?
9. How should safety-critical proprietary components be handled?
10. Could public diagnostic tools become infrastructure like standardized test equipment?
11. What economic models support independent module manufacturers?
12. What would a sustainable public technology commons actually require?
