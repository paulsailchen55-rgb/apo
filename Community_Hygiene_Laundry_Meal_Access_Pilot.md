# Community Hygiene, Laundry & Meal Access Pilot

## Board of Supervisors Proposal Package

**Project status:** Concept development / feasibility research  
**Proposed location:** TBD  
**Purpose:** County feasibility review and pilot development

## Executive Summary

The Community Hygiene, Laundry & Meal Access Pilot proposes a universal, low-barrier community service providing private showers, clothing washing and drying, hygiene access, food preparation, meals, and clothing support in a compact building and parking-lot setting.

The concept is intended as practical public infrastructure rather than a traditional shelter. Access would not require proof of poverty, shelter enrollment, gym membership, or religious affiliation. Potential users include people living outdoors or in vehicles, people between housing, people whose homes lack adequate plumbing, travelers or disaster-displaced residents, emergency laundry users, and any community member who needs the services.

The proposed pilot would combine a small staffed building with a movable or semi-permanent hygiene/laundry pod on an approved hard surface. The technical system could include solar power, battery storage, water storage and treatment, laundry equipment, shower equipment, and water-recovery or wastewater-reduction technologies. All public operation would remain subject to professional engineering review, applicable permits, environmental-health requirements, wastewater authorization, accessibility requirements, fire/life-safety requirements, insurance, and other agency approvals.

### Water Recovery and Wastewater Caution

Water recovery should be treated as an engineered treatment and reuse question, not as an assumption that wastewater simply disappears. Any recovered water would need to be treated and reused only for purposes permitted by applicable standards and approvals.

Solar evaporation can reduce liquid wastewater but produces concentrated residue such as brine, sludge, crystals, or other solids. Evaporation does not guarantee that all contaminants remain behind. Volatile chemicals or fine droplets could move with vapor, so vapor testing, aerosol controls, condensation, and appropriate disposal procedures may be required.

A saltwater battery is an energy-storage concept. It does not automatically consume wastewater or convert wastewater into usable energy. Any design using treated wastewater as an electrolyte would require a separate engineered battery design, safety evaluation, and regulatory review.

For these reasons, the project should be described as a **solar-powered hygiene and laundry unit with water recovery and possible near-zero-liquid-discharge treatment**, rather than claiming that wastewater disappears or becomes energy without testing.

## Safety and Regulatory Requirements

Because the facility would be open to the public, the pilot should be reviewed by appropriate planning, building, fire, environmental-health, wastewater, accessibility, and other authorities before operation.

### Shower Safety

The design should consider:

- Non-slip surfaces.
- Accessible entrance and turning space.
- Grab bars and seating where appropriate.
- Emergency call/intercom capability.
- Door operation from inside.
- Privacy.
- Anti-scald controls.
- Ventilation.
- Adequate lighting and backup lighting where required.
- Daily inspection and cleaning.
- Medical-emergency procedures.
- Procedures for contaminated clothing.

### Water Safety

The system should include, as applicable:

- Separate clean-water and used-water plumbing.
- Backflow prevention.
- Monitoring and treatment verification.
- Filtration and disinfection.
- Safe ozone operation if ozone is used.
- Aerosol controls.
- Overflow alarms.
- Sealed tanks and cleanouts.
- Laboratory testing where required.

Shower or treated wastewater systems should not be treated as a substitute for any required food-handwashing sink.

### Food Safety

Food preparation would likely require applicable food-facility review and should include appropriate handwashing, food-contact surfaces, refrigeration, cooking, ventilation, cleaning, pest control, and waste procedures.

### Waste Handling

Brine, sludge, crystals, filters, lint, contaminated solids, and other residual materials should be contained and handled through approved waste-disposal channels. No wastewater or residue should be discharged onto pavement, soil, landscaping, or storm drains.

### Power and Fire Safety

Solar, wiring, and battery systems should be designed and installed by qualified professionals. The project should include appropriate fire detection/extinguishing measures, thermal protection, emergency shutoffs, vandalism and vehicle-impact protection, and firefighter access.

## Operating Procedures

### Opening

Daily opening checks should cover equipment condition, water systems, power, safety equipment, supplies, cleanliness, and access controls.

### User Access

Appointments or check-in should welcome users without unnecessary personal information. Users should receive clear safety rules, access instructions, shower/dryer instructions, and emergency procedures.

Clothing restrictions may be necessary for needles, gasoline, solvents, pesticides, asbestos, large amounts of feces, unknown hazardous chemicals, or other materials that could damage equipment or endanger users and staff. Unsafe items should be refused or isolated through a defined safety procedure.

### Closing

Closing procedures should include stopping appointments for cleaning, securing the site, checking tanks and residue, checking battery and power systems, cleaning and disinfecting equipment, removing trash or contaminants, recording maintenance, securing supplies, and arranging overnight security.

## Broader Infrastructure Addition: Modular and Flexible Infrastructure

The proposal also asks the County to consider a broader infrastructure model based on **portable, modular, repairable, and reconfigurable systems**.

This could include the use of flexible spaces and microleasing arrangements so that public-serving infrastructure can be deployed where it is needed without requiring every community need to wait for a new permanent building.

Showers, laundry, public kitchens, food services, sanitation, emergency services, and other community infrastructure could potentially be designed as modular systems that can be expanded, repaired, reconfigured, relocated, or redeployed as needs change.

This approach could improve resilience during emergencies, housing disruptions, extreme weather, and changing economic conditions. It also creates an opportunity to test services at smaller scale, measure results, repair or redesign components, and relocate or expand infrastructure when community needs change.

The underlying principle is not that permanent construction is inappropriate. Permanent facilities remain suitable for many public needs. Rather, future public investment could also consider flexible infrastructure where modularity and relocation can reduce deployment time, support resilience, allow incremental expansion, or adapt to changing needs.

A potential lifecycle is:

**Need identified → flexible space → modular unit → short-term deployment → test → measure → repair/reconfigure → relocate or expand.**

Potential modules could include:

- Showers.
- Laundry.
- Public kitchens.
- Food distribution.
- Refrigeration.
- Sanitation.
- Charging.
- Cooling and heating.
- Emergency communications.
- Storage.
- Health/outreach support.

Potential design standards could include:

- Replaceable components.
- Standardized connections.
- Accessible service panels.
- Modular replacement.
- Interchangeable equipment where practical.
- Movable water, wastewater, and power systems where feasible.
- Temporary or permanent placement options.
- Expansion by adding modules.
- Relocation and redeployment capability.
- Emergency and seasonal deployment capability.

## County Resilient Modular Infrastructure Framework

As a longer-term policy concept, the County could study a framework organized around:

1. **Flex Space Infrastructure** — identifying spaces that can host temporary or modular public services.
2. **Modular Public-Service Units** — developing standards for portable service modules.
3. **Mobile Emergency Infrastructure** — equipment that can be deployed during disasters, extreme weather, housing disruptions, or other emergencies.
4. **Repairability Standard** — encouraging public equipment that can be maintained and repaired through replaceable components rather than requiring complete replacement.
5. **Deployment Instead of Construction** — evaluating whether certain needs can be met through deployable infrastructure before committing to a permanent construction project.

## Recommended Project Description

The project can be described as a **universal community hygiene and meal-support station** providing private showers, single-user laundry, clothing support, food preparation and meals, and hygiene supplies. The concept uses a movable, solar-supported, self-contained system with treated-water recovery and sealed residual-waste handling, subject to professional engineering review and all applicable approvals. Operations would use scheduled, trained staff, insurance, regular cleaning, accessibility measures, and approved wastewater and residual-waste procedures.

## Long-Term Vision

The long-term concept is to treat hygiene, clean clothing, food, and dignity as shared community infrastructure rather than services available only through housing status, income verification, institutional enrollment, membership, or affiliation.

The modular infrastructure concept extends that vision by asking whether some public services can be designed to move, scale, repair, and adapt as community needs change.
