# Stage 4 — Exploratory Conversations and Engagement Protocol

## Purpose

Stage 4 begins structured conversations with stakeholders to gather information, identify concerns, discover opportunities, and test the community-microleasing framework. The conversations are exploratory: participation does not constitute endorsement, legal authorization, or a promise that a particular outcome will be adopted.

## Historical and Social Context

Stakeholder engagement should recognize that Santa Cruz County contains overlapping histories and relationships, including Indigenous communities, Spanish and Mexican historical periods, longstanding local families, agricultural and rural communities, students, travelers, visitors, housing-insecure people, businesses, nonprofits, and public institutions. Background research should document relationships to places and activities without treating identity or history as a basis for judging a person’s worth, rights, or credibility.

Historical claims should be distinguished from oral histories, community memories, contested interpretations, and facts requiring verification. The project should avoid treating Santa Cruz County as a single uniform community and should work through geographic and institutional differences.

## Stakeholder Background and Relationship Mapping

For each stakeholder category, document relevant history, relationship to the place, prior collaborations, institutional role, practical knowledge, concerns, and potential opportunities. The project should profile relationships to the decision—not the character or value of the individual.

Relevant categories may include:

- Indigenous communities and cultural organizations
- Longstanding families and local residents
- Farmers, agricultural workers, and rural communities
- Students, educational institutions, and temporary residents
- Travelers, visitors, and seasonal populations
- Businesses, property owners, and property managers
- Artists, musicians, vendors, and informal economic participants
- Nonprofits, neighborhood groups, and community organizations
- Government agencies and public institutions
- People experiencing homelessness or housing insecurity
- Potential users of temporary or flexible spaces

## Conversation Ground Rules

- Explain that the project is gathering information rather than requesting premature approval.
- Do not promise access, permits, funding, or specific policy outcomes.
- Invite participation without requiring it.
- Provide accessible, non-digital, and language-accessible options where possible.
- Record concerns and opportunities accurately, including disagreement.
- Distinguish personal opinions, organizational positions, legal requirements, and formal decisions.
- Do not represent exploratory interest as endorsement.
- Protect personal information and obtain permission before attributing comments by name.

## Initial Conversation Questions

1. What is your relationship to this place, activity, or community?
2. What relevant history or prior experience should be understood?
3. What concerns might flexible space access create?
4. What opportunities might it create?
5. What conditions would make a proposal workable or unacceptable?
6. What authority do you or your organization actually hold?
7. Which claims require legal, historical, or technical verification?
8. Who else should be consulted, including people who are often not represented?
9. What communication methods would make participation practical?
10. What should be documented before any formal decision is made?

## Documentation Record

Each conversation should, where appropriate, record:

- Date and location or geographic area
- Stakeholder category and participant role
- Topic, activity, and relevant space
- Stated authority or relationship to the issue
- Concerns, opportunities, conditions, and recommendations
- Legal or factual claims requiring verification
- Unresolved questions and disagreements
- Consent for attribution and privacy classification
- Follow-up action and responsible person

The original statement should be preserved separately from any summary or categorization so that later analysis does not silently rewrite the participant’s meaning.

## Disagreement and Accountability

Different perspectives should be recorded separately rather than reduced to majority-by-volume. The project should identify which person or institution has formal authority, which participants provide advisory input, and which matters require independent review. The process should not create secret blacklists, retaliation, permanent exclusion based on temporary disagreement, or informal agreements that trade away another person’s lawful rights.

## Potential Pilot Identification

A possible pilot should be assessed for lawful authorization, clear time and operating conditions, manageable impacts, accessible communication, reversibility, time limits, diverse stakeholder participation, measurable outcomes, and independence from a single gatekeeper. A pilot should be treated as a testable and reviewable activity, not as proof that the entire framework has been validated.

## Follow-Up Workflow

1. Thank participants and clarify any misunderstandings.
2. Verify legal, historical, and technical claims.
3. Classify findings by authority, concern, opportunity, and evidence status.
4. Update the stakeholder map and issue register.
5. Identify conflicts, missing perspectives, and unanswered questions.
6. Schedule additional conversations where appropriate.
7. Preserve the distinction between participation, endorsement, and formal authorization.

## Stage 4 Outputs

Expected outputs include a contact log, updated stakeholder map, issue and concern register, authority matrix, candidate pilot list, record of unresolved questions, and a recommendation for Stage 5. The project should also document whether the engagement process adequately included people who lack institutional access, reliable internet, transportation, or confidence in formal systems.

## Unresolved Questions

- Should initial engagement begin in one subregion or use countywide sampling?
- Who can formally authorize a pilot in each proposed location?
- How should anonymous input be handled?
- What evidence is sufficient to describe community preference?
- How should consent and operating conditions change over time?
- How should urgent or same-day requests be handled during exploration?
- How can established institutions be included without overrepresenting them?
- What shared platform could preserve contributions from web, text, phone, mail, and in-person channels?

## Guiding Principle

Find the appropriate time, place, conditions, and responsible parties for an activity rather than treating the existence of a problem as a reason to deny access everywhere.
