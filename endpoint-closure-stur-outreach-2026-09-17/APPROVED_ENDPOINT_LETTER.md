# Approved Endpoint Letter

**Subject:** A simple proposal: every system should have a usable endpoint

Hello,

I am writing with a simple public-policy proposal:

Every system that creates a relationship, obligation, record, or dependency should provide a clear and practically usable way for that relationship to be corrected, transferred, completed, or ended.

We are very good at creating systems that let people enter.

We are much less consistent about helping people leave.

This problem appears in both physical and digital life.

If I am holding an unwanted product, I should be able to determine what I can legally and practically do with it: repair it, reuse it, return it, recycle it, recover its materials, or dispose of it.

If I have an account, identity record, subscription, telephone number, database entry, or other digital relationship, I should likewise be able to understand how to maintain it, correct it, transfer it, or close it.

The important word is practically.

A webpage, telephone number, database, or formal procedure does not necessarily constitute a usable pathway if an ordinary person cannot realistically navigate it.

I believe this can be evaluated with five simple questions:

SPACE — Where is it?

TIME — What clock governs it?

USE — What is it being used for?

RESPONSIBILITY — Who is responsible?

CLOSURE — What happens when it ends?

I call this framework STUR: Space, Time, Use, Responsibility.

The addition of closure is important because modern systems often contain many different clocks and authorities at once. A person may simultaneously encounter government deadlines, licenses, contracts, phone numbers, accounts, databases, corporate policies, and legal obligations — each with its own rules and expiration dates.

The result can be unnecessary administrative exhaustion.

I believe government has a responsibility not merely to regulate these systems, but to make it reasonably possible for ordinary people to understand and exercise their rights within them. This should also protect people who do not use particular technologies.

A person should not have to adopt a particular technological system merely to protect themselves from the consequences of someone else's use of that system.

The standard should protect both the participant and the non-participant.

The underlying principle is simple:

The ability to say yes should have a corresponding ability to say no.

The ability to begin a relationship should have a corresponding ability to end it.

I am not proposing one technological solution. I am asking whether government and regulated institutions can begin evaluating systems according to a basic human question:

“When an ordinary person is finished with this system, can they actually get out?”

If the answer is no, that should be treated as a design and policy problem — not simply as a failure of the individual trying to navigate it.

I would appreciate your consideration of this idea and any guidance about where such a proposal would be most appropriately directed.

Thank you,

Santa Cruz County, California


An easy way out is not necessarily an easy way out of responsibility. It can be an easy way out of a particular arrangement.

Someone can be deeply committed to their principles, their family, their community, their faith, or their work while still saying:

“This particular contract, subscription, debt, account, license, or institutional relationship is no longer working. I need a lawful way to end it.”

Commitment should not be measured by how difficult it is for a person to leave a particular system.

A system should not manufacture permanence merely by making its own termination difficult.

Instead of:

problem → create obligation → create administration → create infrastructure → create more obligations → administer the administration

we can have:

problem → identify responsibility → resolve it → close it → reset.

A system can become so invested in preserving the machinery of an existing arrangement that it fails to notice when the machinery itself has become the problem.

So the question becomes:

Does the system have a legitimate purpose for continuing this person's obligation, or has the obligation itself become the thing the system exists to preserve?

And ultimately:

Can a system provide closure without making the person pay for the system's inability to let go?
