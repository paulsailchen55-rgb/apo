# Community Microleasing — Development and Engagement Plan

## Status

**Classification:** Potential project / framework and applied research direction

**Purpose:** Explore whether indoor and outdoor spaces can be made available through short, clearly governed periods of use, ranging from approximately one hour to conventional leases.

This is a planning document, not a claim that the proposed model is currently legally available.

## Guiding Model

Flexible access may include:

- One-time sessions of approximately 45–60 minutes
- Repeating hourly or daily reservations
- Short-term pop-up use
- Seasonal or multi-month arrangements
- Conventional commercial or property leases

Each arrangement should identify the space, time, permitted activity, responsible party, condition requirements, payment or sponsorship terms, and applicable permissions.

## Development Stages

### Stage 1 — Define the Framework

- [ ] Define “microleasing” and distinguish it from sidewalk vending, pop-up tenancy, permits, and ordinary leases.
- [ ] Establish the intended users: individuals, informal groups, nonprofits, small businesses, artists, service providers, and community organizations.
- [ ] Define possible spaces: storefronts, vacant commercial units, parking areas, plazas, sidewalks, community rooms, and private outdoor areas.
- [ ] Define the minimum record for every session: location, time, user/operator, activity, authorization, fees, and condition/cleanup responsibility.
- [ ] Record exclusions and safety limits rather than assuming every activity can occur in every space.

### Stage 2 — Research Existing Systems

- [ ] Document Santa Cruz sidewalk-vending rules and designated vending areas.
- [ ] Research Downtown Pops! and other vacant-storefront activation programs.
- [ ] Review commercial lease practices, renewal terms, security deposits, insurance, and common minimum terms.
- [ ] Identify zoning, building, fire, accessibility, noise, sanitation, and business-license requirements.
- [ ] Compare Santa Cruz city, Santa Cruz County, private-property, and state jurisdictional roles.
- [ ] Record examples from other cities without assuming they transfer directly to Santa Cruz.

### Stage 3 — Stakeholder Mapping and Engagement

- [ ] Identify city planning, economic development, parks, public works, licensing, and code-compliance contacts.
- [ ] Identify county agencies relevant to health, fire, transportation, and public property.
- [ ] Identify downtown property owners, managers, business associations, nonprofits, and community organizations.
- [ ] Prepare neutral interview questions about barriers, costs, liability, scheduling, and vacant-space activation.
- [ ] Conduct conversations as information gathering, not as commitments or promises of approval.
- [ ] Preserve meeting dates, participants, documents, and follow-up actions.

### Stage 4 — Design a Small Pilot Model

- [ ] Select one hypothetical space type and one low-risk activity.
- [ ] Draft a one-page session agreement.
- [ ] Draft opening, closing, cleaning, incident, cancellation, and dispute procedures.
- [ ] Define who authorizes use and who can terminate a session.
- [ ] Develop a transparent fee, subsidy, or nonprofit sponsorship model.
- [ ] Test accessibility and non-phone alternatives.
- [ ] Obtain legal, insurance, and agency review before any real-world pilot.

### Stage 5 — Evaluation and Expansion

- [ ] Measure utilization, cost, cancellations, incidents, cleanup, user diversity, and repeat demand.
- [ ] Evaluate whether short sessions reduce barriers compared with conventional leases.
- [ ] Identify unintended effects, including displacement, nuisance, exclusion, surveillance, and unequal access.
- [ ] Decide whether to revise, pause, expand, or abandon the pilot model.
- [ ] Document lessons in a reusable framework for other locations.

## Open Questions

- Can a private owner voluntarily authorize hourly use without creating an unintended tenancy?
- What minimum insurance and liability protections are required for different activities?
- How should accessibility, restrooms, electricity, storage, and emergency access be handled?
- How can the system support people without phones, bank accounts, or established businesses?
- Who administers scheduling, disputes, payment, and records?
- What protections prevent the framework from becoming another exclusionary permit system?
