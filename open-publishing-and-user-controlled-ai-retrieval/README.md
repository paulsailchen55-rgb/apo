# Open Publishing and User-Controlled AI Retrieval

## Status

Potential independent project / cross-project framework and future-internet research concept. This archive records an observation about the direction of the internet and a possible alternative architecture. It is not a completed technical or legal specification.

## Central proposition

Imagine an open publishing layer where people can publish text, links, documents, feeds, and other material with minimal procedural friction. Instead of requiring every publisher to predict every possible audience, interpretation, or use, readers access the material through a user-controlled interface—potentially an AI or bot—that retrieves, filters, organizes, summarizes, and presents only what the user requests.

The publishing layer and the viewing interface would be separate. A person could browse directly when permitted and desired, but the primary experience could be machine-assisted retrieval rather than opening every original page in a conventional browser.

The project explores whether this arrangement could preserve broad expression and discoverability while reducing advertising overload, hostile scripts, unnecessary account bureaucracy, tracking, and the burden of manually navigating an increasingly protected and complicated web.

## Access model under consideration

The publishing service would primarily need to establish that a requesting client is an authorized bot or AI interface. It would not necessarily require the service to determine the reader's physical location, age, identity, or personal characteristics merely to provide access to the retrieval layer.

A separate question is whether direct human access should be available through a special interface or controlled pathway. This should not be assumed to be solved by location-based blocking or by requiring publishers to identify every reader. Access policies would need to account for safety, privacy, accessibility, abuse prevention, and applicable law without turning the system into a universal identity or surveillance registry.

## Design direction

- Broad publishing availability, subject to clearly defined baseline constraints.
- User-directed retrieval rather than centrally imposed recommendation.
- Local or user-controlled filtering wherever practical.
- Clear separation between source publication, indexing, retrieval, and presentation.
- Support for RSS, Atom, JSON, JSON-LD, APIs, accessible HTML, documents, and other machine-readable formats.
- Human-readable access remains possible where the service and source permit it.
- No assumption that relevance, legality, popularity, or usefulness can be decided perfectly in advance.
- Preserve source attribution, provenance, corrections, and uncertainty.
- Avoid making a single platform the permanent authority over what information people may discover.

## Historical observation

The author remembers earlier internet environments in which publishing and distribution appeared less burdened by accounts, advertising systems, platform rules, and layers of protective software. Search bots, download tools, aggregators, and filtering tools could perform much of the work of finding and extracting information for a user.

Over time, security concerns, unauthorized access, copyright disputes, commercial protection, advertising systems, tracking, and platform governance contributed to a more restricted and technically complicated environment. This document records that as a personal observation and research starting point, not as a complete historical claim.

## Relationship to other APO work

This concept intersects with the Public Information Commons, AI and Human Information Infrastructure, WAYFINDER, persistent user-controlled online identity, and the Immediate Community Action Network. It should remain conceptually distinct until its technical, social, and legal scope becomes clearer.

## Open questions

- What is the minimum interface required for a publisher to accept bot or AI retrieval?
- How can a system support open publication without enabling malware, unauthorized access, or unlawful distribution?
- How can copyright, privacy, and source permissions be represented without requiring every user to navigate excessive bureaucracy?
- How can users control filtering without making the interface opaque or manipulable?
- What should happen when a source blocks automated access, requires authentication, or provides unclear reuse terms?
- Can the system offer useful human access without requiring universal identity, age, or location collection?
- How can parental controls be implemented at the user or household interface without forcing every publisher to identify every child?
- What governance prevents the retrieval layer from becoming another centralized censorship or surveillance system?
