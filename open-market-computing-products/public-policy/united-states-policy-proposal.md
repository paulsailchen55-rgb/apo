# United States Policy Proposal

## Working title

Open Market Computing Products

## Central policy question

> Does a healthy competitive market in consumer computing require meaningful availability of products that owners are legally and technically permitted to modify, repair, study, and develop for?

## Proposed parallel-market principle

The policy concept does not require every electronic product to be open.

Instead, it asks whether the United States should preserve or encourage a parallel category of genuinely open consumer computing products alongside ordinary proprietary products.

A manufacturer could continue selling closed, integrated products while also offering at least one qualifying open product in an appropriate computing category.

## Possible policy tools

The research should compare, rather than assume, possible approaches:

- voluntary standards;
- federal procurement preferences;
- tax or other incentives;
- competition policy;
- interoperability requirements;
- repairability requirements;
- consumer rights concerning firmware and software;
- documentation requirements;
- protections for lawful modification and research;
- exemptions from technological protection measures for non-infringing activities;
- requirements or incentives for manufacturers to offer qualifying open products.

## Agencies and institutions to investigate

The eventual outreach map should examine, among others:

- Federal Trade Commission;
- U.S. Department of Justice Antitrust Division;
- U.S. Copyright Office;
- Library of Congress;
- Congress and relevant committees;
- Federal Communications Commission, where communications equipment is involved;
- National Institute of Standards and Technology;
- state governments and state right-to-repair programs.

The appropriate institution will depend on the specific policy mechanism.

## Research questions

- What rights does a purchaser currently have to modify computing hardware?
- How do copyright and technological-protection laws affect firmware replacement?
- When does a firmware lock become a competition issue?
- What does interoperability law already require?
- Which restrictions have legitimate security or safety purposes?
- Can openness be defined in a legally workable way?
- Could government encourage open products without dictating product design?
- What existing federal and state policies could be extended?
- What evidence exists concerning education, technical literacy, repair, entrepreneurship, and independent innovation?

## Framing

The proposal should be presented as a market-structure and consumer-choice question rather than as an accusation against technology companies.

The intended goal is to investigate whether proprietary and open computing markets can coexist and whether policy can preserve meaningful room for independent experimentation.

## Status

Potential public-policy project. Research required before drafting legislation or formal petitions.
