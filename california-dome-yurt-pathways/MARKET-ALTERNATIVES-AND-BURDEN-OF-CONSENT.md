# Market Alternatives and the Burden of Consent

## Status

- **PROPOSED:** Research and design direction
- **Scope:** California dome/yurt pathways, existing-home transitions, historic-building stewardship, and related material choices
- **Not:** A contractor estimator, product advertising tool, legal conclusion, or automatic rejection of all synthetic materials

## Core concern

A market may appear open while practical choices are constrained by supplier availability, contractor habits, code interpretations, time pressure, financing, insurance, permitting, and disposal infrastructure. A person may technically be free to choose but lack affordable, understandable, and timely alternatives.

The project should therefore make alternatives easier to discover and compare without becoming an estimator that promotes the products, investments, or preferred assumptions of a particular vendor.

## Design principle

> Do not merely report that the market's opportunities are limited. Identify who bears the limitation, what it costs, which requirements are mandatory, what alternatives exist, and what would be needed to make those alternatives practical.

## What the project should provide

1. **Existing-market awareness:** Identify products, suppliers, contractors, disposal facilities, and established pathways that already exist.
2. **Alternative pathways:** Search for repair, reuse, salvage, local fabrication, traditional methods, modular replacement, material substitution, and producer take-back options.
3. **Burden disclosure:** Record purchase, installation, maintenance, separation, transport, disposal, remediation, and future replacement burdens where evidence is available.
4. **Requirement separation:** Distinguish legal requirements, engineering requirements, insurer requirements, contractor preferences, supplier recommendations, and assumptions.
5. **Time protection:** Encourage a pause for documentation, comparison, second opinions, and written explanations before irreversible commitments.
6. **Agency support:** Give residents and communities understandable choices rather than presenting one commercially convenient pathway as the only possible pathway.

## Anti-estimator boundary

The project is not intended to produce a vendor-centered estimate that embeds a company's products, margins, investment interests, or advertising priorities into the answer. Cost information may be included when it helps expose a burden or compare alternatives, but the tool should not pretend to provide a complete bid or guarantee a final project price.

The preferred output is a **choice-and-burden brief**, such as:

- What is being proposed?
- What is mandatory versus optional?
- What evidence supports the recommendation?
- What alternatives were checked?
- What is the expected end-of-life pathway?
- Who pays for future removal, separation, transport, treatment, or disposal?
- What remains unknown?
- What decision should pause until independently verified?

## Burden of consent

Consent is weakened when a person must decide quickly without understandable information, realistic alternatives, or knowledge of future liabilities. The project should document this as an observable decision condition rather than assume improper intent.

Relevant indicators include:

- Short or unexplained deadlines.
- Claims that a product is the only possible option without supporting documentation.
- Failure to distinguish code requirements from preference.
- No disclosure of end-of-life handling or disposal costs.
- No opportunity for independent review.
- Unclear responsibility for future removal, repair, or hazardous disposal.
- Cost structures that make alternatives appear impossible without showing the assumptions.
- Accessibility, language, income, or survival pressures that limit meaningful participation.

## Producer and system responsibility

The project should investigate whether producers, distributors, installers, developers, agencies, and waste systems disclose the full life-cycle burden of products. Extended producer responsibility is one existing policy model that places some end-of-life responsibility on producers and can encourage design changes; however, existing programs cover particular product categories and should not be assumed to cover building products generally.

## Evidence discipline

Every claim should be labeled as **KNOWN, CALCULATED, TESTED, PROPOSED, SPECULATIVE, or UNKNOWN**. Facility acceptance and disposal prices must be verified for the specific material, condition, quantity, location, and date. A disposal difficulty should not automatically be described as hazardous waste without technical and regulatory confirmation.

## Initial research questions

- Which building materials have producer take-back or stewardship programs?
- Which building materials have no practical take-back pathway?
- What disposal or recovery costs are shifted to homeowners, contractors, local governments, or future owners?
- How often are alternatives rejected because of actual code requirements versus supply-chain convenience?
- What historic-building suppliers, salvage networks, craftspeople, and local fabrication options remain in California?
- How can a resident document a claim that a product is required and request the supporting authority?
- How can the tool present alternatives without endorsing an unverified material or creating a false equivalence?

## Safety boundary

Lower plastic content, natural origin, local production, or historical precedent does not by itself establish structural safety, fire safety, seismic performance, moisture compatibility, indoor-air quality, accessibility, code compliance, or environmental safety. Alternatives must be assessed as complete assemblies in their actual context.
