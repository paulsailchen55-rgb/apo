# Hard-to-Discuss Notes

This file preserves questions that are important to the project but may be difficult to discuss without accidentally turning them into claims, religious arguments, political demands, or assumptions about other people's identities.

## Sanctification of identity

The phrase "sanctification of our ID" emerged as a description of the user's September 19 reflection.

In this context it means examining an identity, cleaning up its representation, restoring what is sound, separating what should not remain attached, and considering what kind of presence should be carried forward.

It is a religiously meaningful phrase for the originator of the inquiry, but it need not become a requirement of the technical system.

## Who am I and who is my neighbor?

The project is partly motivated by the practical implications of the questions "Who am I?" and "Who is my neighbor?"

If people are expected to love, help, communicate with, or take responsibility toward one another, some mechanism of recognition and discovery is necessary.

At the same time, recognition should not automatically mean surveillance.

## Help and discoverability

A discoverable identity can make it easier for people to find someone who can help them. It can also allow systems to infer what a person needs help with.

Those two possibilities are not identical. The first can serve human connection; the second can become profiling or unwanted inference.

The project should therefore examine how discoverability can be useful without making personal vulnerability into an extraction opportunity.

## Religious provenance and public design

The inquiry was undertaken in a Sabbath/rest context and draws from the user's religious understanding of God, neighbor, blessing, sanctification, and renewal.

A future public technical document should distinguish these foundations from claims about what every person, government, search engine, or platform must believe.

## Global scope

The Internet is international. Any proposed identity architecture should therefore account for people with different languages, cultures, religions, calendars, laws, citizenship statuses, and expectations about anonymity.

The project should seek common technical and human principles without assuming that one cultural practice can simply be declared universal.

## Calendar and authority

The conversation raised a desire for an authority regarding timing that is grounded in nature rather than merely in competing human calendars.

This is intentionally left unresolved.

A future investigation can ask whether natural cycles provide sufficiently common reference points for human systems while recognizing that all measurement, naming, and scheduling systems involve human conventions.
