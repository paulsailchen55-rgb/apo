# Nonprofit Operator and Accountability Model

## Purpose

This document describes possible ways a nonprofit or community organization could operate the mobile food-and-water station. It is a planning framework, not legal advice. The exact requirements must be confirmed with the local environmental health agency, the property owner, insurers, and qualified legal or employment advisors before operation.

## 1. Possible operating arrangements

### A. Nonprofit-operated service

The nonprofit owns or leases the equipment, obtains required approvals, establishes standard operating procedures, and operates the service through employees and/or volunteers.

The nonprofit should designate:

- An accountable program manager.
- A food-safety and sanitation lead, as required by the applicable food category.
- A water-system and equipment lead.
- A volunteer coordinator.
- A records and incident-reporting contact.

### B. Nonprofit with a paid employee

The nonprofit hires an individual as an employee to operate the station. The organization defines duties, training, schedules, supervision, safety procedures, reporting, and compensation. Employee classification, payroll, workers' compensation, wage-and-hour, and other employment obligations must be addressed under applicable law.

A paid employee is not automatically a contractor merely because the work occurs at a temporary site or because the nonprofit is charitable.

### C. Nonprofit with an independent contractor

The nonprofit may contract with an outside individual or business for defined services, such as equipment operation, training, logistics, sanitation support, or program management. The agreement should be written and specify scope, compensation, insurance, reporting, confidentiality, ownership of records, incident procedures, and termination rights.

Classification must be evaluated based on the actual relationship and degree of control, not only the wording of the contract. California and federal agencies may determine that a worker is an employee even when the organization labels the person an independent contractor. Obtain professional advice before using this structure.

### D. Volunteer-operated service

The nonprofit may recruit and train volunteers to perform approved duties. Volunteers should sign an acknowledgment describing the mission, role limits, safety expectations, confidentiality, supervision, and incident-reporting process. Volunteers should not be expected to improvise food-safety, medical, water-quality, or emergency decisions beyond their training.

The organization should verify that its insurance covers volunteers and the specific activity. A person who is called a volunteer but is actually working under circumstances that create an employment relationship may create legal risk for the organization.

### E. Partner-operated service

One nonprofit could own or fund the equipment while another nonprofit operates it under a written memorandum of understanding. The agreement should identify the responsible entity for permits, food sourcing, water safety, training, insurance, records, maintenance, cleanup, and complaints.

## 2. Core principle: authorization follows responsibility

The station should not operate based only on informal permission from an individual volunteer. There should be a clear chain of responsibility:

1. Property owner or manager authorizes use of the site.
2. Local enforcement agencies determine applicable food, water, event, sanitation, accessibility, and other requirements.
3. The nonprofit or operating organization accepts operational responsibility.
4. A named supervisor authorizes each service session.
5. The on-site operator follows written procedures and has a way to stop service when a safety condition is uncertain.

## 3. Required information access

The operator must be able to retrieve current information before dispensing food or water. The information system should not depend on a person's memory or on finding a kitchen worker after a question is raised.

For each food packet or ingredient, maintain:

- Product name and unique product ID.
- Complete supplier ingredient statement.
- Major-allergen declaration.
- Cross-contact or shared-facility information when supplied.
- Lot or batch number.
- Shelf-life or use-by information.
- Storage and preparation requirements.
- Supplier contact and verification date.
- Any approved warnings or restrictions.

For water service, maintain:

- Equipment identification.
- Source and production session information.
- Treatment and sanitation records.
- Required test results and verification status.
- Storage and dispensing conditions.
- Maintenance and filter-change records.
- Incident and corrective-action records.

Information should be available through three routes:

1. A phone-accessible page or QR/NFC link, when desired.
2. A compact printed receipt or ingredient record.
3. Direct assistance from the trained operator, including reading the information aloud when requested.

The receipt should identify what was actually dispensed. It should not imply that ingredient disclosure is a guarantee that a person will not have an allergic reaction.

## 4. Recommended service-session workflow

### Before opening

- Confirm property authorization and permitted operating hours.
- Verify that required permits, registrations, and approvals are current.
- Inspect the unit, power system, water system, heating equipment, and dispensing components.
- Confirm sanitation supplies, handwashing facilities, waste handling, and emergency supplies.
- Verify that food products are within approved storage and shelf-life conditions.
- Confirm that ingredient and batch records are available.
- Confirm that the operator and volunteers have completed required training.

### During service

- Explain that ingredient information is available without requiring a phone.
- Record the selected products or otherwise preserve a reliable dispensing record.
- Provide the ingredient receipt or digital record before or at the time of distribution.
- Answer questions using the verified information system rather than guessing.
- Avoid diagnosing allergies or promising medical safety.
- Stop dispensing any product with missing, contradictory, expired, or uncertain information.
- Monitor temperature, sanitation, tampering, equipment alarms, and crowd safety as applicable.
- Record incidents and corrective actions.

### Closing

- Stop service using the approved shutdown procedure.
- Secure food, water, records, equipment, and waste.
- Record remaining inventory, maintenance needs, and incidents.
- Clean and sanitize according to the approved procedure.
- Report any event that requires supervisor, regulator, insurer, or medical follow-up.

## 5. Food-law questions to resolve locally

California has separate statutory categories for nonprofit charitable temporary food facilities, limited-service charitable feeding operations, mobile food facilities, and other food operations. The applicable category may depend on whether the service distributes only original prepackaged foods, heats or assembles commercial ingredients, handles potentially hazardous food, or prepares food on site.

Before a pilot, ask the local enforcement agency in writing:

- Which legal category applies to the proposed unit and menu?
- Is registration, a health permit, plan review, or a temporary-food permit required?
- What handwashing, warewashing, restroom, wastewater, and waste requirements apply?
- What food-safety certification or training is required for the operator?
- Can volunteers perform each proposed task, and what supervision is required?
- What rules apply to atmospheric-water-generated drinking water?
- What records and testing must be maintained?
- What restrictions apply to the site, hours, sound, and temporary setup?

## 6. Suggested minimum written agreements

### Operator agreement

- Parties and legal names.
- Service location and schedule.
- Defined duties and prohibited duties.
- Training and qualification requirements.
- Food, water, sanitation, and emergency procedures.
- Information-access and receipt requirements.
- Incident reporting and authority to stop service.
- Confidentiality and data minimization.
- Insurance and indemnification provisions reviewed by qualified counsel.
- Compensation, if applicable.
- Term, termination, and record return.

### Volunteer agreement

- Volunteer status and role description.
- Orientation and required training.
- Supervisor and escalation contact.
- Safety and conduct expectations.
- No medical diagnosis or unsupported allergy assurances.
- Confidentiality and privacy expectations.
- Incident reporting.
- Confirmation of applicable insurance coverage.

### Property-use agreement

- Exact area and hours of use.
- Equipment placement and access routes.
- Noise, crowd, accessibility, and customer-impact rules.
- Power, water, waste, and restroom arrangements.
- Insurance and responsibility allocation.
- Cleanup, damage, security, and emergency contacts.
- Conditions for suspension or cancellation.

## 7. Practical pilot recommendation

For an initial pilot, use a nonprofit as the accountable operating entity, designate one trained paid or volunteer supervisor, and use additional volunteers only for clearly defined support duties. Begin with the simplest legally approved menu—preferably commercially packaged ingredients and limited preparation—until the local authority confirms what additional food handling is permitted.

The pilot should demonstrate one nonnegotiable service standard:

> No food or drink is distributed unless the operator can immediately identify what was dispensed and provide that information in a phone-based, printed, or human-assisted format.

This standard should be included in the nonprofit's operating policy, training materials, supplier agreements, inspection checklist, and incident-review process.
