# Lease Harm Analysis

This folder develops a focused concept for analyzing whether a lease, lease clause, policy, or enforcement practice can create burdens or harms for the people subject to it.

It is a research and design archive, not a legal determination. The presence of a possible conflict or harm does not establish that a landlord, housing authority, agency, or other institution violated the law. Jurisdiction, facts, governing documents, evidence, and applicable procedures must be checked separately.

## Core question

> What happens when a rule intended to protect a housing program, property, resident, or institution creates a burden, barrier, conflict, or unintended harm for the person who must live under it?

The analyzer should not begin by assuming that the clause is either protective or harmful. It should examine the clause, its purpose, its authority, its actual effects, and possible alternatives.

## Analysis path

1. **Capture the original account**
   - Preserve what the resident actually said or provided.
   - Do not require institutional vocabulary.
   - Preserve uncertainty, chronology, emotion, and unanswered questions.

2. **Identify the governing language**
   - Lease clause
   - Addendum
   - House rule
   - Program requirement
   - Agency policy
   - Statute or regulation
   - Enforcement notice or instruction

3. **State the stated purpose**
   - What does the document say the provision is intended to accomplish?
   - If the purpose is inferred rather than stated, label it as an inference.

4. **Map the burden or possible harm**
   Consider separately:
   - accessibility barrier
   - disability-related burden
   - health or safety concern
   - financial burden
   - time or administrative burden
   - housing-stability risk
   - procedural conflict
   - contractual conflict
   - conflict with another rule or requirement
   - privacy concern
   - retaliation or coercion concern
   - collective-action concern

5. **Separate claim types**
   A useful record distinguishes:
   - direct observation
   - resident-reported experience
   - documented fact
   - interpretation
   - allegation
   - hypothesis
   - legal question
   - verified legal requirement
   - disputed statement
   - unknown

6. **Check for competing obligations**
   A clause may interact with other requirements. The analysis should identify whether the person is being asked to satisfy incompatible, unclear, or practically conflicting obligations.

7. **Consider accommodation, modification, or alternative implementation**
   Where relevant, record possible alternatives without assuming that any particular alternative is legally required.

8. **Identify evidence needed**
   Examples include:
   - complete lease and amendments
   - notices
   - correspondence
   - dates and timelines
   - photographs or other records
   - accommodation requests and responses
   - applicable regulations and policies
   - witness accounts
   - records showing actual consequences

9. **Route the issue**
   Identify who may have authority to answer each question or provide a remedy, while preserving the original account and provenance.

## Design principle

The system should be capable of reaching any of these states:

- no apparent harm identified
- burden identified but cause unclear
- potential harm requiring evidence
- documented conflict requiring review
- accommodation question
- procedural problem
- disputed interpretation
- unresolved

It should not force every difficult experience into a conclusion of illegality or misconduct.

## Why this matters

A lease is not experienced only as text. A resident experiences the interaction between the written rule, their circumstances, other obligations, enforcement practices, and the practical consequences of compliance.

An AI-assisted analyzer can make those interactions easier to inspect, but it should preserve the distinction between **what happened, what the person believes happened, what the documents say, what the evidence establishes, and what remains a question for qualified human review**.
