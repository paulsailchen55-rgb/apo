# The Housing Subsidy Stack
## A Systems Feasibility Framework for Santa Cruz County

**Status:** PROPOSED / RESEARCH IN PROGRESS  
**Geographic focus:** Unincorporated Santa Cruz County, California  
**Date:** September 2026

---

## Abstract

Housing policy is often discussed as if individual requirements can be evaluated independently: increase density, require affordable units, charge impact fees, provide a density bonus, obtain subsidies, or reduce parking.

A development project, however, experiences these requirements simultaneously.

This paper proposes treating the combination as a **housing feasibility stack**:

**LAND → ZONING → DENSITY → DEVELOPMENT STANDARDS → FEES/EXACTIONS → CONSTRUCTION → FINANCING → SUBSIDIES → AFFORDABILITY REQUIREMENTS → MARKET REVENUE → APPROVAL TIME/RISK → FEASIBILITY**

The question is not simply whether each individual policy has a legitimate purpose. The practical question is whether the combined system leaves enough economic capacity for projects to be financed and constructed.

Santa Cruz County's 2026 inclusionary feasibility study provides a useful starting point. The County reports that Keyser Marston Associates (KMA) found a 12% low-income inclusionary requirement supportable by its zoning-compliant rental development prototypes. KMA also modeled density-bonus scenarios in which higher nominal requirements, combined with additional density, produced approximately 11–12% affordable units as a share of total units.

The finding does **not** establish that 12% is the universally correct policy rate. It establishes something more useful for systems analysis: inclusionary policy is being evaluated through a development-feasibility model, and the affordability requirement interacts with the rest of the project economics.

The proposed next step is therefore to model the entire stack rather than search for a single magic percentage.

---

# 1. The Problem

A housing project does not experience an inclusionary requirement in isolation.

A developer must simultaneously account for:

- land acquisition;
- allowable density;
- height and floor-area limitations;
- parking;
- site improvements;
- utility capacity;
- construction costs;
- architecture and engineering;
- permits and fees;
- taxes and insurance;
- financing costs;
- developer return requirements;
- market rents or sale prices;
- affordable-unit requirements;
- density bonuses and concessions;
- subsidies;
- construction and approval timelines;
- market risk.

Changing one component can change the economic capacity available for another.

For example, increasing allowable density can increase potential revenue-producing units. A density bonus can create additional units in exchange for affordable units. A subsidy can close a financing gap. A fee can consume development capacity. Faster approval can reduce carrying costs and financing risk.

The system therefore behaves less like a list of independent rules and more like an interconnected constraint network.

---

# 2. The Subsidy Stack

The term **subsidy stack** should be used broadly here.

It does not mean only a government check.

A project can receive economic capacity from several sources:

### Direct financial support
- grants;
- tax credits;
- below-market loans;
- local housing funds;
- state programs;
- federal programs.

### Regulatory capacity
- additional density;
- increased height;
- reduced parking;
- reduced setbacks;
- concessions;
- streamlined or ministerial approvals.

### Infrastructure capacity
- existing roads;
- existing sewer;
- existing water;
- existing transit;
- existing emergency-service capacity.

### Market capacity
- rents;
- sale prices;
- demand;
- employment access;
- location value.

### Cost reductions
- lower construction cost;
- standardized building systems;
- resilient design;
- reduced utility consumption;
- reduced maintenance;
- reduced infrastructure burden.

A project can therefore become more feasible without receiving a conventional subsidy if another component of the stack reduces its cost or increases its productive capacity.

---

# 3. What the 2026 Santa Cruz County Study Tells Us

Santa Cruz County has released 2026 feasibility and nexus studies prepared by Keyser Marston Associates. The County states that these studies evaluate its affordable-housing inclusionary requirements and Affordable Housing Impact Fee, with additional outreach and Housing Advisory Commission work planned before proposed ordinance or fee changes later in 2026.

KMA's inclusionary feasibility study modeled rental development prototypes.

One important result is that the study found a **12% low-income inclusionary requirement supportable by zoning-compliant rental development** under its assumptions.

The study also examined density-bonus alternatives. Among the reported scenarios:

| Scenario | Requirement / Structure | Affordable share of total units | Stabilized return on total investment |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Base feasibility | 12% low-income | 12.0% | — |
| Density-bonus scenario | 15% of base-zoning units | 12.0% | 4.88% |
| Density-bonus scenario 1 | 20% + density bonus | 11.1% | 4.75% |
| Density-bonus scenario 2 | 20% + density bonus | 11.8% | 4.80% |

**Important:** These are results of KMA's prototypes and assumptions. They are not universal market returns and should not be treated as a prediction for every project.

The study also identifies the lack of market-rate multifamily rental development in unincorporated Santa Cruz County over multiple real-estate cycles as a relevant feasibility constraint.

That makes the production question especially important.

# 4. The Production Question

A policy can be evaluated in at least two different ways.

### Per-project objective

How many affordable units does each successful project produce?

### System objective

How many affordable units does the entire development system produce over time?

Those objectives can diverge.

Suppose a higher affordability requirement increases affordable units **per project**, but also causes some otherwise feasible projects not to proceed.

The resulting system could produce:

- more affordable units per completed project;
- fewer total projects;
- fewer total market-rate units;
- fewer total affordable units;
- or some combination.

The opposite can also occur: a lower requirement combined with more density, faster approvals, lower fees, and financing support could allow more projects to proceed.

The appropriate empirical question is therefore:

> **What combination produces the greatest feasible housing output while achieving the desired affordability and public objectives?**

That question can be tested.

---

# 5. The Stack as a Binding Constraint

A useful working hypothesis is:

> **Housing production may be constrained not by one rule, but by the cumulative interaction of multiple rules and costs.**

Call this the **Binding Stack Hypothesis**.

The project becomes infeasible when the combined burden exceeds the economic capacity generated by the site.

Conceptually:

**Project Capacity − Land − Construction − Financing − Fees − Standards − Affordability Burden − Risk = Residual Feasibility**

The residual must be sufficient for financing and the required return.

This is not a legal formula. It is a research model for organizing the variables that need to be measured.

---

# 6. Why “We Just Need Developers” Is Not a Complete Feasibility Strategy

There is a legitimate practical concern behind the statement that communities need developers who will actually build.

But the statement raises a second question:

> **What conditions allow developers to actually build?**

California HCD itself recognizes financial feasibility as part of housing planning. HCD guidance identifies market demand, financial feasibility, development experience, available financing, land, infrastructure, fees, development standards, and processing constraints as relevant to housing production.

HCD also identifies policy tools that can reduce or offset constraints, including:

- density bonuses;
- incentives and concessions;
- fee reductions or waivers;
- reduced parking;
- flexible development standards;
- streamlined processing;
- public land;
- state and federal financing;
- local financing;
- support for applications to housing funds and tax-credit programs.

This is important because it places the subsidy-stack concept inside an existing housing-policy framework rather than treating it as an entirely new theory.

---

# 7. The Role of Resilient Housing

A further research question is whether resilient housing can change the economics of the stack.

For example, a housing system might be designed to reduce:

- utility demand;
- water demand;
- sewer demand;
- transportation demand;
- fire exposure;
- maintenance costs;
- replacement costs;
- infrastructure dependence.

A dome or other unconventional housing form should **not** be assumed to receive automatic fee reductions merely because it is resilient.

Instead, the proper question is:

> **Can measurable reductions in public infrastructure demand or project costs be documented, and do the applicable fee statutes, nexus studies, and ordinances recognize those differences?**

That is a testable proposition.

# 8. The Local-to-State-to-Federal Ladder

The investigation should proceed upward in levels rather than beginning with an assumption that the problem must be solved nationally.

## Level 1 — Project

Can a specific housing design pencil?

Measure:

- units;
- square footage;
- land;
- construction;
- fees;
- financing;
- affordable units;
- operating costs;
- revenue.

## Level 2 — County

Which County requirements consume project capacity?

Measure:

- inclusionary percentage;
- Affordable Housing Impact Fee;
- other impact fees;
- parking;
- height;
- density;
- processing time;
- infrastructure requirements.

## Level 3 — California

Which state requirements shape the local stack?

Measure:

- density-bonus law;
- Housing Element requirements;
- inclusionary-housing rules;
- streamlining laws;
- state financing;
- state building requirements.

## Level 4 — Federal

Which federal programs provide financing or constraints?

Potential areas include:

- Low-Income Housing Tax Credits;
- HOME;
- CDBG;
- FHA-related financing;
- federal housing programs;
- tax policy.

## Level 5 — Capital Markets

Only after the public-policy layers are documented should the analysis move into:

- interest rates;
- construction lending;
- permanent financing;
- insurance;
- institutional investment;
- risk pricing;
- credit conditions.

This prevents the investigation from jumping from a local fee question to an unsupported global explanation.

# 9. A Possible Systems Solution

There may not be one definitive solution.

The more plausible structure is a **balanced feasibility package**.

Instead of asking:

> “Should the inclusionary percentage be higher or lower?”

ask:

> “What complete package of density, standards, fees, financing, subsidies, and affordability requirements produces the target number of affordable and total housing units?”

A package might be modeled as:

**More productive density**
+
**appropriate inclusionary requirement**
+
**density bonus**
+
**lower or better-targeted fees**
+
**faster approvals**
+
**existing infrastructure**
+
**public/state/federal financing**
+
**lower-cost construction**
+
**resilient design**
=
**greater feasible housing production**

Whether this equation works is an empirical question.

# 10. Questions for the Board, Housing Advocates, and Developers

These questions are deliberately neutral and measurable.

### Question 1
What is the target number of affordable units the County expects the inclusionary program to produce over a defined period?

### Question 2
How many total projects are expected to remain financially feasible under the proposed requirement?

### Question 3
What happens to total affordable-unit production if the requirement changes from 10% to 12%, 15%, or 20%?

### Question 4
How does the answer change when density, height, parking, fees, financing costs, and approval time are changed simultaneously?

### Question 5
What is the marginal cost of producing each additional affordable unit through the inclusionary program?

### Question 6
Could the same public objective be achieved with a different combination of density, subsidy, fee reduction, public land, or financing?

### Question 7
Which constraints are legally fixed, which are locally adjustable, and which require state or federal action?

### Question 8
What real projects have failed to proceed because of cumulative feasibility constraints, and which specific constraint was binding?

### Question 9
What would make an innovative resilient housing prototype economically feasible without assuming that it should receive an exemption?

### Question 10
What measurable evidence would cause the County to change the inclusionary requirement in either direction?

---

# 11. What We Do Not Yet Know

This paper does **not** establish:

- that inclusionary requirements are too high;
- that impact fees are unlawful;
- that resilient housing should be exempt from fees;
- that subsidies are sufficient or insufficient;
- that a particular percentage is optimal;
- that state or federal policy is the primary cause of local housing costs;
- that capital markets are intentionally preventing housing production;
- that any particular developer would build a particular project under a changed policy.

Those questions require additional evidence.

The purpose of this paper is to identify the system that needs to be measured.

---

# 12. Research Program

The next phase should build a transparent feasibility model.

### Model A — Baseline
Current zoning + current fees + current inclusionary requirement.

### Model B — Density
Baseline + increased density.

### Model C — Fee reform
Baseline + altered fee structure.

### Model D — Inclusionary scenarios
10%, 12%, 15%, and 20%.

### Model E — Full stack
Density + inclusionary + fees + parking + financing + approval time.

### Model F — Resilient prototype
Full stack + documented infrastructure/operating-cost differences.

For every model, record:

- total units;
- affordable units;
- market-rate units;
- total development cost;
- public subsidy;
- developer equity;
- debt;
- annual revenue;
- operating cost;
- return;
- estimated feasibility;
- infrastructure demand;
- time to approval/construction.

The goal is not to find a preferred political answer.

The goal is to identify **which variables actually bind**.

---

# 13. Preliminary Finding

### FINDING — PROPOSED / SUPPORTED BY CURRENT DOCUMENT REVIEW

The Santa Cruz housing question appears more accurately described as a **systems-feasibility problem** than as a single inclusionary-percentage problem.

The 2026 County/KMA work already demonstrates that affordability requirements are being evaluated in relation to development feasibility and density-bonus structures. California HCD likewise identifies financial feasibility, development standards, fees, infrastructure, financing, and regulatory constraints as relevant components of housing production.

Therefore, the most useful next step is not to argue for a particular percentage.

It is to build a reproducible model showing:

> **How much housing—and how much affordable housing—each configuration of the subsidy stack can actually produce.**

That is the point at which the discussion can move from competing intuitions to measurable system performance.
