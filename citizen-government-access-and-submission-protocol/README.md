# Citizen–Government Access and Submission Protocol

## Classification
Research question / international public-infrastructure concept

## Core observation

An ordinary citizen may have a legitimate question, policy proposal, concern, or piece of public-interest information without knowing which governmental institution has jurisdiction or which submission format that institution accepts.

Modern governments provide many participation mechanisms—constituent correspondence, public comments, petitions, hearings, consultations, complaints, web forms, email, postal correspondence, and specialized portals—but these mechanisms are fragmented.

The practical problem is therefore not simply whether government allows participation. It is whether an ordinary person can **understand how to enter the system** without already understanding the system.

## Central research question

**Does the modern world need a universal citizen-to-government submission protocol?**

This does not necessarily mean one global website or one centralized database. It may mean a common underlying protocol by which a citizen can identify:

- what kind of submission they are making;
- what jurisdiction they are in;
- which institutions may have relevant authority;
- what formats are accepted;
- what supporting materials can be attached;
- whether the submission becomes public;
- what privacy choices exist;
- whether a response is expected;
- and where the submission can be routed if the citizen initially chooses the wrong institution.

## Accessibility problem

The citizen should not have to become an expert in governmental organization before being able to communicate with government.

Potential barriers include:

- unclear jurisdiction;
- different forms and procedures;
- different file and document requirements;
- inconsistent publication and privacy rules;
- unclear routing between agencies and legislative bodies;
- different requirements at local, state, national, and international levels;
- technical literacy requirements;
- disability and accessibility barriers;
- language barriers;
- socioeconomic barriers;
- uncertainty about whether an ordinary policy proposal is even an accepted category of communication.

A person who repeatedly encounters these barriers may simply stop participating.

## Important distinction

Fragmentation can have legitimate purposes. Distributed authority can provide checks and balances, prevent a single institution from controlling every decision, and allow different jurisdictions or agencies to respond to different kinds of problems.

The research question is therefore **not** whether all government should become one system.

The question is whether **access to government authority can be made simpler without eliminating legitimate separation of authority.**

## Possible design principle

> **Government authority may remain distributed while citizen access to that authority becomes easier to navigate.**

A common submission protocol could function as a routing layer rather than a replacement for government institutions.

## Example citizen flow

Citizen:

> “I have a new policy idea concerning consumer technology and ownership.”

The system could identify:

1. jurisdiction;
2. subject area;
3. potentially relevant legislative bodies;
4. potentially relevant regulatory agencies;
5. research or standards institutions;
6. existing public-comment or consultation processes;
7. appropriate submission formats;
8. privacy/publication consequences.

The citizen could then choose where to submit rather than having to discover the entire governmental structure independently.

## Document-format question

This project should specifically investigate whether governments have sufficiently clear standards for ordinary submissions, including:

- PDF requirements;
- page limits;
- single- or double-sided documents;
- accessibility requirements;
- accepted file formats;
- links and external websites;
- attachments;
- electronic signatures;
- naming conventions;
- scanning and optical-character-recognition requirements;
- publication of submitted materials;
- treatment of AI-assisted documents;
- requirements that differ between email, web forms, postal mail, and formal proceedings.

A citizen should not have to learn these rules accidentally through repeated trial and error.

## International scope

This is not only a United States question.

The research should compare:

- local governments;
- U.S. state governments;
- U.S. federal government;
- other national governments;
- regional institutions;
- United Nations institutions;
- international organizations;
- existing open-government and e-participation frameworks.

Existing work on citizen participation and digital government should be mapped before proposing a new standard.

## Working hypothesis

Modern governments may have substantially improved their ability to process information while still leaving the **citizen's path into the information system fragmented and difficult to understand**.

That hypothesis should be tested rather than assumed.

## Relationship to the Open Market Computing Products project

This research began while trying to determine how an ordinary citizen could submit a policy proposal concerning computing products, repair, interoperability, intellectual-property timelines, and independent experimentation.

It is now treated as a separate project because the underlying problem applies far beyond computing products.

## Possible future project

**Citizen–Government Submission Protocol (CGSP)**

A potential open specification describing a minimum set of information and routing metadata that governments could support without requiring governments to surrender their separate jurisdictions, procedures, or legal authorities.

This is only a research direction at present.

## Guiding question

**If a citizen has something legitimate to say to their government, should finding the correct door be almost as difficult as understanding the problem they are trying to solve?**

## Archive boundary

This folder records the observation and research question. It does not yet claim that a universal protocol is legally, technically, or politically feasible.

The next stage is comparative research into systems that already exist.
