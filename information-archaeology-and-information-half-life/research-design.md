# Research Design

Research question: Does the practical discoverability of public information decline with age, and is there any meaningful pattern around a ten-year interval?

Secondary questions:
1. How often do original pages disappear or change substantially?
2. How often do archived copies survive when originals do not?
3. Does ordinary search retrieve historical material less effectively than specialized archives or direct source searches?
4. How much does search-result displacement by newer pages affect recovery of older information?
5. Do different kinds of information have different survival curves?
6. What proportion of apparent disappearance can be explained by link rot, domain expiration, redesigns, platform closure, access controls, or search-index changes?
7. Can documented deliberate removal be distinguished from ordinary information decay?

Ten-year hypothesis: the ten-year idea is a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion. Possible observations include gradual decline, step changes caused by platform or policy events, or no special ten-year boundary. Compare multiple age bands rather than looking only for ten-year examples.

Suggested protocol: choose historical topics with dated material. Record a source at or near its original publication date, then attempt recovery using ordinary search, exact-title or exact-phrase search, direct URL retrieval, recognized archives, bibliographic indexes, later citations, and legitimate independent copies. Record successful and failed routes. Do not count a search-engine ranking position as proof that a source has been deleted.

Proposed data fields: source_id, topic, source_type, original_date, original_url, current_status, archive_status, ordinary_search_found, specialized_search_found, independent_copy_count, access_barrier, last_verified_date, evidence_notes.

Outcome categories: Normally discoverable; Search-degraded; Archive-dependent; Secondary-only; Unrecovered; Documented removal. The final category should be evidence-based.

LED health evidence test: build parallel collections of historical public claims and scientific evidence, then compare what is recoverable today with what was available at the time. Do not equate existence of a historical claim with scientific validity.

Search-result displacement question: measure results screened before reaching a historical source, proportion of top results that are commercial/SEO pages, time-to-historical-source, exact-phrase versus broad-query success, and general-search versus archive/bibliographic routes. Do not attribute the effect to a particular company or intentional strategy without evidence.

Reproducibility: preserve date, query, source type, successful path, failed paths, and archive identifiers for each experiment.
