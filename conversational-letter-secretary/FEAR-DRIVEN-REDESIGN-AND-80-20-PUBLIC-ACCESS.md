# Fear-Driven Redesign and the 80/20 Public-Access Hypothesis

## Status

**PROPOSED / CONCEPTUAL RESEARCH NOTE**

## Purpose

This note records a broader concern arising from the Conversational Letter Secretary project: systems designed to prevent abuse can become increasingly difficult for ordinary people to use when institutions redesign access around feared or possible threats.

The concern does not require that the feared threat be proven to be the majority cause of the redesign. A possibility, uncertainty, or minority risk can still influence institutional decisions, technical architecture, and user requirements. The resulting burden on ordinary people is real even when the underlying explanation remains disputed or weakly evidenced.

## Core Observation

Repeated authentication, account verification, anti-abuse screening, recovery requirements, and provider restrictions can consume substantial human time before a person completes a basic action such as sending an email.

The project should examine the cumulative burden rather than evaluate each verification step in isolation. A step may be defensible by itself while the overall sequence becomes excessive, confusing, inaccessible, or disproportionate to the risk of the action.

## Fear, Uncertainty, and Institutional Adaptation

**PROPOSED RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS:** Institutions may redesign systems not only in response to demonstrated harm, but also in response to uncertainty, anticipated threats, public pressure, liability concerns, and fear of what automated systems or bad actors might do.

This hypothesis must be investigated case by case. The project should distinguish:

- Documented incidents and measured abuse.
- Credible but uncertain risk assessments.
- Institutional precautionary decisions.
- Public speculation, fear, or political and commercial pressure.
- Actual effects on ordinary users.

The existence of a precautionary response does not itself establish that the feared scenario is common, proven, or the principal cause of the policy.

## Human Time as a Protected Resource

Authentication burden should be evaluated as a resource and accessibility issue. Relevant costs include:

- Time spent repeatedly authenticating.
- Loss of attention and task continuity.
- Dependence on a working phone number, current device, or specific biometric method.
- Difficulty recovering an account.
- Data, financial, mobility, disability, or technical barriers.
- Discouragement from participating in ordinary communication or civic life.

Security should protect people without treating every ordinary user as an assumed threat or requiring an impractical number of steps for low-risk actions.

## 80/20 Public-Access Economic Hypothesis

**PROPOSED / EXPERIMENTAL:** Explore whether approximately 80 percent of basic online knowledge, access, communication, and civic-use infrastructure should be freely available, while approximately 20 percent may support commercial, institutional, or revenue-generating activity.

The 80/20 ratio is a design hypothesis and communication tool, not a universal law or a demonstrated optimal economic allocation. The project should investigate where such a ratio could be useful, how it could be measured, and where it would fail.

Possible areas of inquiry include:

- Public access to basic information and educational resources.
- Open standards and interoperability.
- Low-cost or free basic communication.
- Public-interest digital infrastructure.
- Commercial services layered on top of accessible foundations.
- Limits, exceptions, and funding mechanisms needed for sustainability and security.

## Pareto Principle Reference

The Pareto principle is a broad observation that unequal distributions sometimes approximate an 80/20 pattern. It should not be treated as a natural requirement that every system must follow an exact 80/20 allocation.

This project proposes applying the pattern as an economic and civic design experiment: test whether a large freely accessible foundation, combined with a smaller commercial or institutional layer, could improve human access while still supporting maintenance, innovation, security, and resilience.

## Constitutional and Civic Question

The project may examine whether an American public-access model could permit a greater commercial share than the proposed 20 percent—for example, a 60/40 arrangement—while still preserving broad public access and the practical ability to participate in civic life.

This is an open constitutional, economic, and policy question. The document does not claim that the Constitution establishes a specific 80/20 or 60/40 digital allocation. Any future analysis should distinguish constitutional text, legal precedent, public-policy proposals, economic assumptions, and personal values.

## Design Principles to Investigate

1. Preserve a freely accessible basic layer for essential knowledge and communication.
2. Make security requirements understandable, proportional, and visible.
3. Avoid repeated verification when a lower-burden trusted authorization method is sufficient.
4. Separate protection against high-risk actions from routine low-risk human activity.
5. Measure the total user burden across the complete workflow.
6. Provide accessible alternatives when a person lacks a phone number, current device, money, or a particular biometric capability.
7. Preserve commercial and institutional sustainability without making basic participation dependent on excessive payment or authentication.
8. Treat human time, attention, and ability to participate as design constraints.
9. Test the proposed ratios rather than assuming that nature, economics, or constitutional principles automatically mandate them.
10. Document both intended protections and unintended consequences.

## Questions for Future Research

- How many authentication steps are required for common low-risk online actions?
- Which steps are based on demonstrated abuse, anticipated risk, legal requirements, liability, or commercial policy?
- How much time do different user groups spend completing authentication and recovery?
- Can risk-based authentication reduce burden without weakening meaningful protection?
- What online services should qualify as a freely accessible public foundation?
- How would an 80/20 model be funded and governed?
- What would change under a 60/40 model, and what tradeoffs would result?
- How should the model account for infrastructure, security, privacy, disability access, and international participation?
- What evidence would show that a proposed allocation improves access rather than merely shifting costs?

## Evidence Labels

- **KNOWN:** Authentication and anti-abuse requirements can impose time, access, and recovery burdens; the size and cause of the burden require measurement.
- **PROPOSED:** Evaluate fear-driven redesign and cumulative authentication burden as system-level issues.
- **PROPOSED:** Explore an 80/20 public-access economic model.
- **SPECULATIVE:** Fear, uncertainty, and concern about AI or bot abuse may influence institutional redesign even when the feared scenario is not proven to be prevalent.
- **UNKNOWN:** The appropriate public/commercial allocation, constitutional implications, funding model, and effects across user populations.
- **TESTED:** No comparative authentication-burden study or 80/20 economic experiment has yet been completed.

## Boundary

This note does not recommend bypassing legitimate security controls or assume that corporations, governments, AI developers, or users have a single motive. It proposes a human-centered investigation of proportional security, cumulative administrative burden, public access, and the unintended consequences of designing systems around fear and uncertainty.
