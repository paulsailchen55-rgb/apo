# Possible Vehicle Concept — Modular Personal Property / Mobility Platform

**Status:** PROPOSED / SPECULATIVE concept archive  
**Date:** 2026-09-21  
**Relationship to project:** Candidate architecture for the Mobile Dignity and Property Support System

## Purpose

This document records a possible vehicle architecture discussed as a design direction, not as a finished specification or current product.

The central idea is to build around a **small, durable, human-powered cargo platform** rather than around a conventional electric scooter or bicycle.

The vehicle would carry a standardized personal-property container, remain useful when the rider is simply walking and pushing it, and accept optional human-powered, hand-powered, and removable electric-assist modules.

## Core concept

The vehicle is envisioned as:

- a stable three-wheel platform;
- a front steering/push structure that can fold or telescope into a comfortable walking push handle;
- reliable hand-operated braking and a parking brake;
- a low cargo deck/platform;
- a removable or fold-out seat;
- an optional reciprocating/treadle propulsion mechanism rather than requiring circular bicycle pedals;
- provision for an optional hand-propulsion mechanism as a separate future module;
- a removable electric-assist module rather than permanently defining the vehicle as electric;
- a standardized lockable personal-property container;
- a telescoping or folding sleeping deck integrated into the lower platform;
- a lightweight deployable tent/bivy shelter;
- optional solar charging infrastructure associated with the shelter/bed module.

The vehicle should remain useful without the electric module.

## Personal-property container

A major design principle is to use an existing, mass-produced suitcase/container rather than inventing an expensive proprietary cargo box.

Suitable containers may be obtained from:

- department stores;
- discount retailers;
- online retailers;
- thrift stores;
- reuse/recovery programs.

The desired characteristics are:

- hard shell;
- lockable;
- water-resistant or weather-resistant construction;
- common replacement availability;
- inexpensive enough to replace rather than repair when economically appropriate;
- dimensions that can be standardized across the vehicle fleet.

### Travel-size reference envelope

There is **no single universal airplane or bus luggage size**. Airline and bus policies vary.

Current examples provide useful design boundaries:

- American Airlines lists a carry-on maximum of **22 x 14 x 9 inches**, including handles and wheels.
- Delta lists the same **22 x 14 x 9 inch** maximum for carry-on baggage.
- Greyhound currently lists a free carry-on limit of **16 x 12 x 7 inches and 25 lb** on its route information.
- Amtrak permits carry-on items up to **28 x 22 x 14 inches and 50 lb** on most services; the Pacific Surfliner uses **28 x 22 x 11 inches**.

Therefore, this project should **not claim that one container is universally bus- or airplane-compliant**.

A useful preliminary design target is instead:

**Primary compact-property container target: approximately 22 x 14 x 9 inches or smaller, with the understanding that actual carrier acceptance must be checked for each trip.**

A larger secondary container could be considered if the vehicle is intended to function as a local mobility/property system rather than as a direct carry-on solution.

## Example container cost

Current retail examples show inexpensive hardshell luggage with integrated combination/TSA locks in approximately the **$40–$100 range**, depending on size, construction, and seller. Current Walmart examples include 20-inch and 24-inch hardshell cases in this general range.

Used/thrift pricing may be substantially lower, but this should be measured rather than assumed.

**Preliminary budgeting allowance:** approximately **$25–$100 per container** for planning purposes, with used/recovered luggage potentially reducing cost.

This is a planning estimate, not a procurement price.

## Rolling platform

The platform should favor:

- three-wheel stability;
- low center of gravity;
- small overall width;
- strong frame;
- repairable bearings and hubs;
- replaceable wheels/tires;
- mechanical simplicity;
- ordinary hand tools for maintenance where possible;
- braking sized for the loaded vehicle, not merely the empty vehicle.

### Wheel concept

A preliminary preference is for **non-pneumatic solid wheels/tires** to eliminate punctures.

Possible technologies include:

- solid rubber;
- polyurethane;
- thermoplastic/rubber compounds;
- cellular/non-pneumatic tires;
- other industrial-style replaceable wheel assemblies.

The design should avoid treating any wheel as literally permanent. Solid wheels can wear, develop uneven wear, transmit more vibration, and have greater rolling resistance than some pneumatic designs.

The important requirement is:

> **No routine flat tires.**

Wheel wear should be inspectable, replaceable, and preferably inexpensive.

A prototype should test several solid-wheel compounds and tread profiles before choosing a production configuration.

## Human propulsion

### Reciprocating/treadle drive

The preferred human-powered mechanism is potentially a **reciprocating pedal/treadle drive**.

Instead of circular bicycle cranks:

- the rider pushes one treadle downward;
- the opposite treadle rises;
- the drivetrain converts alternating strokes into forward wheel rotation;
- gearing can provide different mechanical advantages.

This is a real mechanical design family and should be investigated as a candidate subsystem.

The mechanism should be removable or serviceable independently of the cargo platform.

### Walking mode

The vehicle must remain fully useful without using the treadles.

The user should be able to:

1. stand behind it;
2. grasp the push/steering bar;
3. push it like a durable personal-property cart;
4. steer and brake it normally.

This is a fundamental mode, not an emergency fallback.

## Optional electric assistance

Electric assistance should be a **removable module**.

Initial program vehicles could be completely human-powered.

Electric-assist units could later be assigned according to documented need, accessibility requirements, program rules, or other appropriate criteria.

The module should be:

- removable without rebuilding the vehicle;
- independently serviceable;
- independently replaceable;
- mechanically/electrically protected;
- speed-limited appropriately for the vehicle;
- equipped with a removable battery where practical.

The project should not require every vehicle to have a motor.

### Charging network

If electric vehicles are deployed, the program could establish known charging locations through partner organizations, service centers, storage facilities, or other existing infrastructure.

The charging network should not depend on the user having a permanent residence.

## Integrated sleeping system

The lower cargo platform may also become the foundation of a temporary sleeping system.

A possible sequence:

1. property container is secured;
2. seat is removed or folded;
3. cargo/sleeping deck telescopes outward;
4. additional support legs deploy;
5. lightweight tent/bivy structure unfolds upward;
6. the resulting surface becomes a temporary sleeping platform.

The sleeping module should be designed around the same structural frame rather than being a separate large object carried by the user.

The exact sleeping dimensions remain UNKNOWN and require ergonomic and structural testing.

## Solar charging concept

A possible future configuration would place deployable solar panels on or adjacent to the sleeping/shelter structure.

The concept is:

- open the sleeping system;
- deploy a solar surface;
- solar energy charges the removable battery while the vehicle is stationary;
- close/fold the system when moving.

The solar material should **not be described as inherently fireproof**.

Instead, future research should examine:

- certified photovoltaic materials;
- fire-rated backing/encapsulation;
- protected wiring;
- charge-controller safety;
- battery thermal management;
- separation between PV components and sleeping materials;
- heat dissipation;
- safe deployment during charging.

A solar system should be treated as an optional energy subsystem, not a prerequisite for the basic vehicle.

## Design philosophy

The strongest version of this concept is not a special-purpose homeless cart.

It is a **small personal mobility and property platform** that happens to be useful in situations where people lack secure housing or permanent storage.

The design should therefore emphasize:

- ordinary components;
- ordinary luggage;
- repairability;
- reuse;
- modularity;
- low replacement cost;
- user ownership or durable custody;
- privacy;
- mobility;
- accessibility;
- compatibility with existing infrastructure.

## Candidate requirements for future platform search

When evaluating commercial donor platforms or building a prototype, prioritize:

1. Can it be pushed comfortably while walking?
2. Can the steering/brake controls work from the walking position?
3. Can a seat be added and removed without major structural work?
4. Can a treadle/reciprocating drive be installed?
5. Can a low cargo deck carry a standardized luggage container?
6. Can the cargo container be locked to the frame?
7. Can the frame accept non-pneumatic wheels?
8. Can the electric module be removed?
9. Can the sleeping deck attach without making the vehicle permanently enormous?
10. Can the frame be repaired locally?
11. Can the loaded vehicle be stopped safely?
12. Can the vehicle remain useful when all optional modules are removed?

## Important boundary

This document is a **possibility record**.

It does not establish that the vehicle should be built exactly this way, that any particular commercial product is suitable, or that the resulting vehicle would be accepted on an airline, bus, train, sidewalk, road, or other transportation system.

The next step is comparative prototype research rather than committing to a final design.
