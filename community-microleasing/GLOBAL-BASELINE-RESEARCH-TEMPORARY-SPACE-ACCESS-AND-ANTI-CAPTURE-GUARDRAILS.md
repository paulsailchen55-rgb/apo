# Global Baseline Research: Temporary Space Access, Urban Commons, Flexible Permissioning, and Anti-Capture Guardrails

**Status:** Preliminary research baseline, not a systematic review or legal opinion  
**Date:** 2026-09-20  
**Related project:** Community Microleasing / Flexible Space Access  
**Related concepts:** GIS space inventory, same-day authorization, seasonal reconsideration, nonprofit-supported access, macroleasing, and microleasing

## Executive summary

This paper examines whether the Community Microleasing concept is connected to existing international movements and systems. The answer is yes. Relevant fields include temporary urbanism, meanwhile use, tactical urbanism, urban commons, community land trusts, cooperative ownership, participatory governance, civic permitting, and digital public-sector decision systems.

The proposed concept should not be described as entirely unprecedented. Many of its individual elements already exist. Its potentially distinctive contribution is the attempt to connect them into one accountable infrastructure:

1. A GIS-based inventory of potentially usable spaces.
2. A basic unit of space plus a defined time period, potentially one hour.
3. A database of possible activities and their legal, safety, insurance, accessibility, health, zoning, and operational requirements.
4. Same-day decisions: approved, approved with conditions, human review, not authorized, emergency pathway, or insufficient information.
5. A recorded reason for every denial, with no automatic conversion of a temporary denial into indefinite exclusion.
6. Alternatives involving another space, time, activity, or nonprofit sponsor where lawful and practical.
7. Seasonal and annual accountability so that short-term decisions cannot quietly eliminate an entire seasonal opportunity.
8. Human-operable administration even when AI assists with database construction, discovery, matching, and explanation.
9. Strong safeguards against privatization, speculative capture, favoritism, surveillance, and the concentration of access in already-powerful organizations.

The project is therefore best understood as a proposed **public-interest access and authorization infrastructure**, not merely a booking app and not merely a temporary-urbanism project.

## 1. Scope and research caution

This is an initial baseline assembled from academic literature, institutional materials, and public descriptions of relevant projects. It is not yet a comprehensive global census. The sources use different definitions of temporary use, commons, community ownership, placemaking, and civic permissioning. Their results cannot be treated as directly comparable without additional coding.

A previous discussion referred to a figure of 1,313 policy actions across 524 jurisdictions. That figure must be traced to its original publication and its definitions, date range, inclusion criteria, and geographic coverage before being used as a central quantitative claim. The present paper does not treat that figure as independently verified.

## 2. Existing international fields

### 2.1 Temporary urbanism

Temporary urbanism concerns the short-term use or transformation of urban spaces. It can include vacant buildings, public rights-of-way, plazas, cultural projects, gardens, pop-up commerce, events, and experimental public-space arrangements.

The overlap with Community Microleasing is substantial: both recognize that a space does not have to be permanently assigned to one use. However, many temporary-urbanism projects begin with a particular site and a particular sponsoring organization. The proposed system begins with a more general inventory and asks what lawful uses could occur in a defined place and time.

Important research questions:

- Who chooses which activities are permitted?
- Is the space genuinely open to people with limited money and institutional experience?
- Does temporary use lead to durable access, or merely make an area more attractive for later private development?
- Are local residents decision-makers, consultees, customers, or symbolic participants?
- Does the program preserve the right to say no, and is the reason for no visible?

### 2.2 Meanwhile use

Meanwhile use is the temporary occupation of vacant or underused property before a permanent development or tenant arrangement is established. The UK organization Meanwhile Space has documented projects involving local authorities, landlords, communities, and temporary users.

This field offers practical lessons about agreements, maintenance, insurance, property-owner incentives, and the transition between temporary and permanent use. It also presents a potential danger: temporary use can become a tool for property marketing, area-branding, or speculative value growth without giving participants lasting influence.

The proposed model should therefore distinguish:

- Temporary use for public benefit;
- Temporary use that primarily improves an asset's market value;
- Temporary use that prepares a user for a longer-term lease;
- Temporary use that is intentionally non-speculative and may end without creating a pathway to private capture.

Source: https://www.meanwhile-space.com/

### 2.3 Tactical urbanism and placemaking

Tactical urbanism uses relatively quick, often low-cost interventions to test or change urban space. Placemaking programs may allow temporary installations, pedestrian amenities, neighborhood activities, and improvements to underused public or private areas.

For example, San Diego's 2026 placemaking bulletin defines placemaking as temporary use of public right-of-way and private property to activate streetscapes and support neighborhood-serving activities. The bulletin also describes projects as commonly involving private funding and community-oriented organizations.

This demonstrates an important point: public-space access systems already exist, but they often depend on project-specific applications, funding, and organized sponsors. A person without an organization, insurance resources, or administrative experience may still be excluded.

Source: https://www.sandiego.gov/development-services/forms-publications/information-bulletins/568

### 2.4 Urban commons and commoning

Urban-commons research examines how people collectively manage or reclaim resources that are otherwise enclosed by public or private systems. Examples include community-managed buildings, gardens, cultural spaces, cooperative facilities, and collective land stewardship.

Research on temporary commoning argues that temporary practices can help residents reimagine urban openness, while also recognizing that temporary access may remain limited if the underlying ownership structure does not change.

This distinction is central to the proposed project:

> Temporary access should be capable of producing real use, dignity, learning, relationships, and future opportunity without requiring the participant to become a property owner or a powerful institution.

### 2.5 Community land trusts and cooperative ownership

Community land trusts (CLTs) and related cooperative models address a different time scale: they seek to preserve community control and affordability over the long term. A nonprofit or community-controlled body may own land while individuals or groups hold use or building rights under restrictions intended to prevent speculative displacement.

International research identifies recurring themes:

- Separating land ownership from building or use rights;
- Limiting speculative resale or windfall extraction;
- Giving residents and community members governance roles;
- Using nonprofit or cooperative structures;
- Treating land as a community resource rather than only as a financial asset.

CLTs do not solve the entire microleasing problem because they usually require acquisition, capital, organizational capacity, and long-term stewardship. They are nevertheless important models for the paper's anti-capture design. They show that legal ownership and control can be structured to preserve community benefit rather than maximize short-term asset appreciation.

### 2.6 Digital civic permissioning

A particularly relevant contemporary example is Civic Ledger, which describes an AI-supported permissioning system for cities. Its public description emphasizes structured and auditable workflows for requests involving shared urban assets, risk assessment, conditions, stakeholder coordination, and recorded decisions. It explicitly states that the system supports human decision-making and does not automate final public decisions.

This overlaps with the proposed system in several ways:

- Shared urban assets;
- Fragmented permission systems;
- Conditional agreements;
- Risk evaluation;
- Reusable records and precedents;
- AI as support rather than final authority.

It also means that the proposed concept should be compared with existing civic-tech systems rather than assuming that AI-assisted permissioning is an untouched area.

Source: https://civicledger.city/

## 3. The WeWork comparison and the financial-capture problem

The company likely being recalled in the conversation is **WeWork**, although the identification should be confirmed if a different organization was intended.

WeWork is relevant as a cautionary case, not because temporary or flexible use is inherently defective, but because its financial structure combined long-term lease obligations with short-term customer commitments and aggressive growth expectations. Stanford's case summary states that WeWork filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in November 2023 after rapid global expansion and difficult interactions with financial markets. Contemporary analyses also emphasize governance concerns, high fixed obligations, cash burn, and the mismatch between long-term leases and flexible memberships.

Sources:

- https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/case-studies/wework-unicorn-bankruptcy
- https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/07/wework-bankruptcy-business-bad/
- https://www.thecorporategovernanceinstitute.com/insights/case-studies/what-exactly-happened-to-wework/

The lesson for Community Microleasing is not simply “avoid money.” Money is necessary for maintenance, insurance, staffing, accessibility, technology, and safety. The design problem is whether financial capital becomes the mechanism that determines who may access space, who controls the system, and who captures the value created by participants.

### 3.1 Structural risks to examine

- **Fixed-cost exposure:** An organization takes on long leases or debt and then must maximize occupancy or revenue.
- **Growth pressure:** The system expands before its governance, safety, and financial controls are mature.
- **Asset inflation:** Temporary activity is used to increase the market value of land or buildings, after which original users are displaced.
- **Platform capture:** A private operator becomes the indispensable intermediary and can raise fees, restrict access, or sell behavioral data.
- **Concentrated control:** A founder, investor, landlord, or sponsor can exercise disproportionate authority.
- **Cross-subsidy opacity:** Profitable activities subsidize public-benefit activities, but users and communities cannot see the actual allocation.
- **Mission drift:** A nonprofit or public program gradually prioritizes revenue, political convenience, or institutional survival over access.

## 4. Proposed anti-capture guardrails

These are design hypotheses for research and testing, not guarantees of incorruptibility.

### 4.1 Separate ownership, authorization, operation, and oversight

No single actor should automatically control all four functions:

1. Ownership or legal control of the space;
2. Authorization of a particular activity and time;
3. Operational support, staffing, and maintenance;
4. Independent oversight, complaints, audit, and appeal.

A property owner may control whether a space is available, but should not necessarily control the entire data system, the eligibility rules, the complaint process, and the public reporting.

### 4.2 No pay-to-win access

Payment may cover documented costs, but the system should not allow wealth alone to purchase priority over people with fewer resources. The database should record whether an arrangement is:

- Free;
- Cost-recovery;
- Subsidized;
- Sponsored by a nonprofit;
- Community-benefit compensated;
- Commercial and market-rate;
- Restricted by law or owner conditions.

If priority is given, the reason should be public and reviewable. A person with no money should have a defined pathway to seek sponsorship, a subsidy, a volunteer-supported arrangement, or a lower-cost alternative.

### 4.3 Limit control concentration

Potential safeguards include:

- Term limits or rotation for governance roles;
- Conflict-of-interest disclosures;
- Restrictions on related-party transactions;
- Public records of major contracts and payments;
- Independent approval for system-wide rule changes;
- One-person-one-vote or other non-wealth-weighted governance where appropriate;
- A ban on transferring the public-interest database into private ownership without a public process;
- Requirements that essential access functions remain interoperable and exportable.

### 4.4 Prevent indefinite administrative exclusion

Every denial should record:

- The exact requested space, activity, and time;
- The decision-maker and legal authority;
- The specific reason;
- Whether the barrier is temporary or permanent;
- Missing information, if any;
- A lawful alternative, if one exists;
- A review date or event when reconsideration is appropriate;
- Whether the denial caused a seasonal or time-sensitive opportunity to expire.

“Pending” should not become an unbounded category. If a same-day decision cannot be made, the system should issue a clear outcome under the established rules, while distinguishing an ordinary denial from an emergency or legally required human-review pathway.

### 4.5 Protect against repeated temporary refusals

A series of individually explainable denials can produce permanent exclusion in practice. The system should therefore support aggregate review for patterns such as:

- Repeated denials involving the same person or group;
- Repeated denials involving a particular neighborhood or demographic;
- Requirements that change between applications;
- Delays that repeatedly cause seasonal opportunities to expire;
- Spaces being withheld while owners or institutions receive benefits from the applicant's activity;
- Rules applied more strictly to informal or unhoused participants than to established organizations.

Pattern review must be designed carefully to avoid unnecessary surveillance or stigmatization. Aggregate analysis should use data minimization and access controls.

### 4.6 Keep the system usable without AI

AI can assist with building the knowledge graph, finding relevant rules, identifying possible activities, translating language, and explaining requirements. It should not be necessary for a trained human to operate the system.

The system should provide:

- Human-readable forms;
- Phone and in-person access;
- Paper or accessible alternatives;
- Clear explanations of why a request was denied;
- Manual override only with recorded authority and reason;
- Audit logs for automated recommendations and human decisions;
- A way to correct inaccurate space or rule data.

### 4.7 Data governance and surveillance limits

The system should collect the minimum data needed to authorize and administer an activity. It should distinguish between:

- Public information about space availability and general rules;
- Restricted operational information;
- Personal information about applicants;
- Sensitive information involving homelessness, disability, immigration, health, or safety;
- Audit information needed for accountability.

The system should not assume that vehicle identification, DMV access, facial recognition, location tracking, or continuous monitoring are necessary. Such capabilities would require separate legal, ethical, and public-governance review and should not be built into the basic access model by default.

### 4.8 Preserve public benefit after success

A successful temporary activity should not automatically become a mechanism for displacing the people who created it. Possible safeguards to investigate include:

- Community-benefit conditions;
- Non-displacement commitments;
- Public reporting of later property transactions;
- Right of first negotiation for community organizations where legally possible;
- Community land trust or cooperative pathways for long-term stewardship;
- Limits on converting public subsidies into private windfalls;
- Sunset and renewal reviews based on public benefit rather than only revenue.

## 5. Economic benefits to investigate

The paper should not assume that economic benefits are automatic. It should identify and measure possible benefits and costs.

### Potential benefits

- Better use of vacant or underused space;
- Lower entry barriers for small enterprises and informal creators;
- New pathways for people without property or established credit;
- Reduced administrative duplication;
- Faster matching of needs with available space;
- Support for seasonal commerce and cultural activity;
- Increased local spending and foot traffic;
- Opportunities for nonprofit services and mutual aid;
- Testing before a costly long-term commitment;
- More efficient use of public assets;
- Community learning, social connection, and skill development.

### Potential costs and harms

- Administrative and insurance costs;
- Increased conflict among users, neighbors, owners, and agencies;
- Gentrification or asset-value inflation;
- Unequal access to digital systems;
- Data breaches and surveillance;
- Liability and safety incidents;
- Volunteer burnout;
- Displacement of existing informal users;
- Platform monopolization;
- Unequal enforcement;
- Financial dependence on sponsors whose priorities change.

A serious evaluation should compare both monetary and nonmonetary outcomes, including dignity, access, autonomy, safety, repeat participation, and whether benefits reach people who were previously excluded.

## 6. Global comparative research matrix to build next

A subsequent phase should identify approximately 15–25 concrete programs across regions. Each case should be coded using the same fields:

| Field | Research question |
|---|---|
| Country and city | Where does the program operate? |
| Space type | Public right-of-way, vacant building, park, plaza, land, facility, or private property? |
| User eligibility | Who can apply? Individuals, nonprofits, businesses, informal groups, or only approved entities? |
| Time scale | Minutes, hours, days, months, seasons, or years? |
| Decision process | Staff, elected body, owner, community board, automated recommendation, or mixed? |
| Decision time | Same day, days, weeks, or uncertain? |
| Requirements | Insurance, permits, safety plans, fees, sponsorship, licensing? |
| Cost | Free, cost recovery, subsidy, market-rate, or negotiated? |
| Governance | Public, nonprofit, cooperative, private, or hybrid? |
| Data practices | What is collected, published, retained, or shared? |
| Anti-capture provisions | Are there limits on speculation, concentration, related-party transactions, or displacement? |
| Accountability | Appeals, audits, public reporting, independent review, or none? |
| Outcomes | Documented benefits, exclusions, conflicts, or unintended consequences? |
| Transferability | What could be adapted to Santa Cruz County or a countywide model? |

Suggested geographic coverage:

- United Kingdom and Ireland: meanwhile use, community ownership, civic assets;
- Continental Europe: urban commons, cooperative ownership, temporary use;
- North America: placemaking, parklets, public-space permits, community land trusts;
- Latin America: collective land and community-managed space;
- Africa: informal commerce, community land, and negotiated public-space use;
- Asia: informal urban use, civic access, and digital government systems;
- Australia and New Zealand: public-space activation and community governance.

## 7. Preliminary analytical conclusion

The global movement is not one single standardized movement. It is a network of related practices that address different pieces of the same problem: urban land and facilities are often underused, while people who need space face complex rules, high costs, institutional barriers, and uncertain access.

Existing practices show that temporary access, shared governance, nonprofit stewardship, cooperative ownership, and digital permissioning are all real fields with practical examples. They also show that temporary access can remain shallow if it does not address ownership, decision power, displacement, and financial capture.

The proposed Community Microleasing model is therefore best positioned as a synthesis and extension:

> A public-interest system for matching people and activities with available space and time, while integrating lawful requirements and building safeguards against exclusion, indefinite delay, surveillance, speculative capture, and concentration of control.

Its novelty should be investigated at the level of the **combination and governance architecture**, not claimed merely because each component is new. The strongest research contribution may be the anti-capture design: ensuring that a system created to expand access does not become another gatekeeping institution or a financial platform that converts public participation into private asset appreciation.

## 8. Open questions requiring further research

1. Does any jurisdiction operate a genuinely countywide or citywide inventory of spaces linked to activity-specific, time-based authorization?
2. Does any existing system promise a same-day definite decision rather than indefinite pending status?
3. How are informal users and people without property, insurance, organizational affiliation, or digital access treated?
4. Which legal structures best protect a public-interest access platform from privatization or mission drift?
5. Can community land trusts, cooperatives, public trusts, or benefit corporations provide useful ownership models for the physical assets involved?
6. What forms of payment and subsidy avoid wealth-based priority while still covering real costs?
7. How can the system audit repeated denials without creating a surveillance database of vulnerable people?
8. What independent body should investigate complaints against the platform, property owners, nonprofits, or public agencies?
9. How should the system handle emergencies, seasonal deadlines, weather, festivals, and changing zoning rules?
10. What measures would demonstrate genuine empowerment for a person who has no property and is not represented by an established organization?

## 9. Working design principle

The system should make lawful, safe, temporary access easier without turning access into a privilege reserved for people who already possess money, property, institutional status, or professional administrative support.

It should be designed so that:

- Access is explainable;
- Decisions are time-bounded;
- Denials are specific and reviewable;
- Alternatives are considered;
- Data collection is limited;
- Authority is distributed;
- Money cannot silently become the only source of influence;
- Public benefit survives changes in leadership, funding, ownership, and economic cycles;
- People can participate for a short period without having to prove that they deserve permanent membership in the system.
