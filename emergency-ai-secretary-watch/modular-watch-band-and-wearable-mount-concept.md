# Modular Watch Band & Wearable Mount Concept

## Status

Exploratory physical-interface concept for the Emergency AI Secretary Watch. This is a design possibility, not a final mechanical specification.

## Core idea

The watch should not necessarily be permanently attached to one conventional wristband.

The preferred concept is a **modular watch puck plus flexible attachment system**:

- the watch puck contains the electronics/display and primary interface;
- the band or mount is a separate replaceable component;
- the puck can attach at different positions on the band;
- the same puck can potentially be moved between different wearable mounts.

The goal is to make the computing/communication object useful beyond the definition of a conventional wristwatch.

## Childhood-inspired prototype reference

The inspiration is the older style of flexible metal "slap" bracelet:

- stored approximately flat or curved;
- wraps around the wrist when slapped against it;
- remains attached during ordinary movement and sports;
- can be inexpensive and replaceable.

This is a historical design reference for exploration, not a claim that an existing slap-bracelet mechanism is automatically suitable for a modern electronic device.

## Proposed watch configuration

### Puck

The puck could contain:

- display;
- time/date functions;
- microphone;
- speaker or audio transducer;
- cellular/radio electronics;
- GNSS/GPS hardware as separately controlled;
- physical controls;
- battery;
- attachment interface.

The puck should remain a distinct module rather than being permanently integrated into the band.

### Band

The band could provide:

- wrist attachment;
- mechanical retention of the puck;
- optional tactile or decorative elements;
- attachment points for accessories;
- possible conductive or electronic interfaces if later justified.

The band should itself be replaceable.

## Puck placement

The design should investigate whether the puck can be positioned at different points along the band.

Possible benefits:

- user preference;
- accessibility;
- different wrist positions;
- protection from impacts;
- improved microphone/speaker placement;
- easier viewing;
- alternative wearing styles.

The mechanical interface must prevent accidental release during ordinary activity.

## Beyond the wrist

The puck should be considered a **general wearable communication module that happens to function as a watch**.

Potential mounts include:

- wristband;
- necklace or lanyard;
- clothing clip;
- pin/badge mount;
- bag or equipment mount;
- other purpose-built holders.

Each mount would need its own safety and retention testing.

The watch display remains valuable because it provides an immediate non-voice interface even when voice interaction is inconvenient, noisy, unavailable, or unreliable.

## Band accessories

The band itself could become a platform for nonessential additions:

- decorative patches;
- identification elements;
- accessibility markers;
- tactile markers;
- reflective material;
- small passive accessories;
- interchangeable attachment pieces.

Accessories should not interfere with emergency controls, microphone/audio performance, RF operation, charging, or safe retention.

## Material direction

The band material should be evaluated independently from the electronics.

Candidate categories for research include:

- flexible metal;
- conventional elastomer;
- textile;
- woven or braided material;
- recyclable polymer;
- modular combinations of durable materials.

The project should evaluate:

- skin contact;
- durability;
- sweat/water exposure;
- flexibility;
- fatigue;
- impact resistance;
- retention;
- repairability;
- replaceability;
- material recovery;
- manufacturing complexity;
- cost.

No particular material should be selected merely because it appears environmentally preferable; the complete lifecycle and actual performance should be tested.

## Mechanical design questions

1. How does the puck attach to the band?
2. Can it be removed without tools?
3. Can it be removed intentionally but not accidentally?
4. Can the puck survive sports movement and impacts?
5. Does the attachment mechanism create pressure points?
6. Can the band be manufactured and repaired locally?
7. Can the band be replaced independently?
8. Can the puck use multiple mount types?
9. Can a damaged band be replaced without replacing the electronics?
10. Can a damaged puck be moved to a new band?
11. Can the attachment mechanism be manufactured without specialized proprietary tooling?
12. Does the attachment mechanism remain compatible with the Omega lifecycle requirements?

## Interface philosophy

The device should support two complementary modes:

**Watch mode**
- Look at the display.
- Use physical controls when necessary.
- Enter numbers or other information through tactile controls if voice is unavailable.

**Secretary mode**
- Speak to the puck.
- Let the secretary service handle linguistic, telephone, and computational complexity.
- Receive synthesized speech and/or text responses.

The design should not assume that voice will always work perfectly.

The display and physical controls therefore remain important fallback interfaces.

## Relationship to DTMF and tactile entry

If the project retains a physical number-entry mechanism, the band/puck design should leave room for tactile controls.

The exact mechanism remains open:

- rotating bezel;
- buttons;
- touch interface;
- other tactile selector;
- voice-assisted entry;
- secretary-mediated telephone entry.

The physical interface should be evaluated against the text-first and phoneme research rather than prematurely committing the entire project to one input method.

## Omega Principle connection

The modular architecture supports the lifecycle framework:

> **Replace the band without replacing the puck. Repair the puck without discarding the band. Reuse the puck in another mount. Recover each component at its actual end.**

This is an example of designing the device as a collection of useful components rather than as a single disposable object.

## Prototype sequence

A practical prototype sequence could be:

1. Test a simple slap-style flexible band without electronics.
2. Add a mechanically retained dummy puck.
3. Test retention during walking, running, sports movement, and ordinary impacts.
4. Test removal and intentional repositioning.
5. Test several puck positions.
6. Test alternate mounts such as necklace, clip, and pin.
7. Add an actual electronics puck only after the mechanical interface is satisfactory.
8. Evaluate microphone, speaker, antenna, charging, display, and button performance in each mount.
9. Evaluate band materials and lifecycle characteristics.
10. Document the resulting interface as a candidate watch-design parameter.

## Guiding design principle

**The watch is the puck; the band is one way of wearing it.**

The wristwatch should remain the preferred everyday form, while the underlying architecture should not make the puck useless when the user wants to wear or attach it somewhere else.
