## 1. Purpose

This document updates the California state-level housing analysis after the Governor's 2026 signing and veto activity.

The purpose is **not** to rank bills or determine which policy is best.

The purpose is to identify what California has actually changed, what was rejected, and which changes can now be examined as real-world experiments in the housing transmission system.

The central research question remains:

> **Where does intended housing capacity change as it moves through the California housing system, and what friction, distortion, delay, or loss occurs at each interface?**

The transmission chain remains:

```text
STATE HOUSING NEED
        ↓
STATE REQUIREMENT
        ↓
REGIONAL ALLOCATION / RHNA
        ↓
LOCAL HOUSING ELEMENT
        ↓
LAND + ZONING + SITE CAPACITY
        ↓
DEVELOPMENT STANDARDS
        ↓
PROJECT FEASIBILITY
        ↓
FINANCING
        ↓
PERMITTING / APPROVAL
        ↓
CONSTRUCTION
        ↓
OCCUPANCY / LEASE-UP
        ↓
AFFORDABILITY
        ↓
HOUSING STABILITY
```

## 2. Why the 2026 Signing Cycle Matters

Earlier versions of this research focused heavily on pending legislation.

That is no longer sufficient.

A number of housing-related measures have now been enacted, while at least one measure directly relevant to offsite housing factories was vetoed. This creates a more useful research environment:

1. **Signed law** can be studied as an implementation condition.
2. **Vetoed legislation** can be studied as a boundary of the adopted policy system.
3. **Remaining pending legislation** can still be examined for possible position-letter activity.
4. **Implementation outcomes** can eventually be compared against the state's stated objectives.

The research therefore shifts from:

> "Which bill should we write about?"

toward:

> **"What did California change, what did California decline to change, and where should we measure the transmission of those changes into actual housing outcomes?"**

# 3. Major Enacted Housing Finance Change: AB 179

## 3.1 Status

**AB 179 (2026)** was signed July 13, 2026 as part of the 2026–27 State Budget.

The Governor's announcement describes AB 179 as a housing budget trailer bill that modernizes California's affordable-housing finance system.

The state described the reforms as including:

- modernization of affordable-housing finance;
- "One-Stop Shop" reforms intended to streamline project delivery;
- reduction of duplicative reviews;
- changes intended to reduce affordable-housing development costs;
- impact-fee changes;
- a new Disaster Rebuilding Fund;
- additional state housing investment.

The Governor's announcement estimated that One-Stop Shop financing reforms and impact-fee changes could reduce affordable-housing construction costs by approximately $60,000–$70,000 per unit.

**Evidence tag: KNOWN — enacted bill and Governor's stated description.**

## 3.2 Transmission relevance

AB 179 is particularly relevant because it directly touches multiple interfaces:

```text
FINANCING
   ↓
REVIEW / ADMINISTRATION
   ↓
FEES
   ↓
DEVELOPMENT COST
   ↓
PROJECT FEASIBILITY
   ↓
HOUSING PRODUCTION
```

The research question is not whether the Governor's estimated savings are correct.

The research question is:

> **After implementation, does the reform measurably increase the number of stable affordable homes produced per dollar and per unit of administrative effort?**

## 3.3 Proposed measurement

Track before/after implementation where data permit:

- number of financing applications;
- number of duplicative reviews;
- review duration;
- financing transaction count;
- administrative cost;
- fee burden;
- total development cost;
- state subsidy per completed unit;
- time from application to construction;
- time from construction to occupancy;
- completed affordable units;
- lease-up success;
- continued affordability;
- occupancy continuity.

This converts the reform from a policy description into a transmission experiment.

# 4. Housing Administration as a State-Level Transmission Interface

The Governor's June 30 announcement describes the agency as a coordinated statewide strategy for housing and homelessness, with departments and entities including HCD, Cal ICH, CalHFA, and the Civil Rights Department.

The same announcement reported state-level reductions in development processing time and described a statewide planning capacity of approximately 3.6 million new homes, including 1.4 million affordable units.

# 7. AB 2166 — Offsite Housing Factories: VETOED

## Status

**AB 2166 (2026)** was vetoed by the Governor on September 18, 2026.

The bill concerned:

> Multifamily housing development: offsite housing factories: backstop financing.

The Governor's September 18 legislative update explicitly lists AB 2166 among the vetoed bills.

**Evidence tag: KNOWN — vetoed.**

## Why this matters to the Modular Building Platform Project

AB 2166 is unusually relevant to the project's modular/factory-built concept because it addressed **offsite housing factories** and financing.

The important research point is not that the veto proves offsite construction is good or bad.

The veto creates a boundary condition:

> **California's enacted 2026 policy system contains some support for factory-built / modular delivery, while at least one proposed financing mechanism for offsite housing factories was not enacted.**

That distinction should be preserved.

## Research questions

- What exactly did AB 2166 propose?
- What financing problem was its backstop intended to address?
- What did the Governor identify as the reason for the veto?
- Which portions of the concept already exist under California law?
- Which financing interface remains unresolved?
- Does a factory require a guaranteed pipeline to justify capital investment?
- Does factory capacity create a new bottleneck if land, permitting, utilities, or financing remain slow?
- Can an adaptable building platform use factory production without requiring a permanent single-use market?

# 18. Evidence Rules

For this project, the following distinctions must remain explicit.

### KNOWN
The measure was enacted or vetoed, and the status is supported by an official state source.

### CALCULATED
A quantity is calculated from documented source data.

### TESTED
A claim has been tested against actual project or administrative data.

### PROPOSED
A research metric, model, or design idea created by this project.

### SPECULATIVE
A hypothesis that has not yet been demonstrated.

### UNKNOWN
The available source material does not yet establish the answer.

Do not convert:

**state claim → established fact**

without independent verification when the distinction matters.

# 20. Updated Research Objective

The project should now measure more than whether California "builds housing."

The stronger research question is:

> **How much housing capacity survives the complete transmission from state policy to long-term household stability, and how much administrative, financial, regulatory, construction, and transaction cost is required to preserve that capacity?**

A second question:

> **When California reforms one interface, does friction disappear, or does it move somewhere else in the system?**

A third:

> **When a proposed mechanism is rejected, what unresolved transmission problem remains?**

And for the modular building-platform project:

> **Could a long-lived adaptable building platform reduce the number of major transactions required when housing, parking, workspace, emergency shelter, and community needs change over time?**

# 21. Working Hypothesis

> **California's housing system may be understood as a transmission and feedback system in which state objectives pass through multiple regulatory, financial, technical, administrative, and physical interfaces before becoming occupied and stable housing. The 2026 legislative cycle provides a new opportunity to test whether reforms reduce friction, merely relocate it, or create new interfaces elsewhere.**

This remains a **PROPOSED RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS**, not a conclusion.
