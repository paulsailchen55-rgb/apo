# Adaptive Systems, Intended Use, and Restrictive Feedback Loops

## Status and purpose

Preliminary standalone APO framework. This is a reusable conceptual and research lens for examining how systems respond when real-world needs exceed their original design assumptions.

## Core proposition

A system designed around a narrow definition of intended use may encounter legitimate variations in how people need to use it. When the system cannot accommodate those variations, operators may add restrictions rather than adapt the design. This can exclude legitimate users, create additional problems, and produce a restrictive feedback loop.

> When a system cannot accommodate legitimate real-world use, it may respond by restricting access rather than adapting its design.

This is a conceptual proposal, not an established scientific law. Claims should be tested through documented examples and comparative research.

## Intended use versus actual use

Distinguish among intended use, legitimate variation, operational strain, abuse or prohibited use, and unaccommodated need. The question is not whether every use should be permitted, but whether restrictions are proportionate, evidence-based, understandable, reviewable, and designed to preserve legitimate access.

## Restrictive feedback loop

1. A system is designed for a narrow intended use.
2. Users encounter needs outside the design assumptions.
3. Some unanticipated uses create genuine operational difficulties.
4. The operator responds with broad restrictions.
5. Legitimate users are excluded along with harmful uses.
6. Excluded users improvise or seek alternatives.
7. New safety, maintenance, visibility, or administrative problems emerge.
8. Those problems are used to justify additional restrictions.

The loop is not inevitable and must be examined case by case.

## Adaptive access

An adaptive system accommodates legitimate variation while retaining proportionate safeguards. Review questions include: What need is unaccommodated? What harm is documented? Can the design be modified? Can different service levels or emergency access be offered? Are rules achievable? Is human review available? Who is excluded and why?

## Application to mobile trikes and storage

A cargo trike may be used for transportation, personal storage, sleeping equipment, and movement between destinations. Facilities designed for ordinary bicycles may not accommodate loaded trikes. An adaptive response could consider larger bays, modified access, reservations, emergency allowances, clear prohibited-material rules, separate cargo storage, subsidized access, and recovery procedures when a vehicle is impounded or separated from its user.

## Governance and mission drift

Public-benefit programs may introduce paid tiers, eligibility requirements, administrative procedures, or commercial partnerships as demand grows. Safeguards to examine include protected public-benefit capacity, separate accounting, transparent allocation, public reporting, community governance, emergency access, periodic review, and mission-protection clauses.

## AI and coordination

AI may coordinate storage, transportation, appointments, forms, property recovery, and referrals across fragmented organizations. It must not become an additional barrier. It should not independently deny emergency assistance, judge worthiness, or expose sensitive location information without authority and oversight. Confirmed availability should be reported only after the responsible organization confirms it.

> One request should lead to one understandable pathway, even when multiple organizations are involved behind the scenes.

## Evaluation criteria

- **Access:** Who can use the system, and what documentation, payment, or approval is required?
- **Adaptability:** Can legitimate variations be accommodated?
- **Proportionality:** Do restrictions address specific demonstrated risks?
- **Continuity:** What happens after detention, hospitalization, displacement, or separation from property?
- **Governance:** Who controls the system, who benefits financially, and are decisions reviewable?
- **Privacy:** What data is collected, who can access it, and how long is it retained?

## Related concepts

Systems thinking; institutional feedback loops; administrative burden; mission drift; design justice; universal design; proportionate regulation; public-infrastructure governance; resilience engineering; human-centered service design; privacy-preserving coordination; and the Omega Principle's concern with a system's proper or honorable end.

## Research questions

1. When do organizations respond to legitimate variation with broad restrictions?
2. How can legitimate variation be distinguished from harmful misuse?
3. What evidence shows that restrictions produce secondary problems?
4. Which governance mechanisms protect public-benefit access?
5. How can emergency access be safe and sustainable?
6. How can AI reduce administrative burden without increasing surveillance?
7. How should exclusion, abandonment, and successful recovery be measured?
8. Can modular infrastructure reduce the need to rebuild systems as needs change?

## Preliminary design principle

> Design systems to accommodate legitimate variation before restricting access. When restrictions are necessary, make them specific, proportionate, understandable, reviewable, and compatible with the system's underlying public purpose.

## Separate duty of consequence awareness

Every established arrangement—an institution, policy, technology, infrastructure, organization, or informal practice—can produce consequences beyond its original intentions. Awareness of those consequences is an ongoing responsibility, not a one-time planning task.

> Establishing a system creates a continuing duty to observe what the system produces, including effects that were not intended at the time of its creation.

This duty includes observing consequences, listening to affected people, noticing invisible patterns, revisiting assumptions, creating time for reflection, and modifying the system when its effects diverge from its purpose.

### Administrative burden and loss of reflection

A system can accumulate procedures, deadlines, compliance requirements, and emergencies until participants lack time or attention to reflect. A possible sequence is: growing responsibilities; reduced reflection; normalized unintended consequences; reduced corrective capacity; and still more procedures. A system may consume the very attention required to determine whether it is functioning well.

### Human and developmental dimension

People may experience reduced reflective capacity as adult responsibilities increase. Childhood can sometimes include more unstructured time for curiosity and observation, while later responsibilities can reduce opportunities for reflection. This is a hypothesis requiring careful, non-universal treatment rather than an established generalization.

### Institutional implications

Useful mechanisms include scheduled reflection, easy feedback, independent evaluation, unintended-effect reports, user and community participation, low-burden concern channels, authority to modify procedures, and protection for people who identify failures.

> Alongside the duty to establish and operate a system, there is a separate continuing duty to remain aware of its unintended consequences through time and space. A system that leaves no time, capacity, or authority for such awareness may become unable to recognize or correct the harms it produces.

## Scope and limitations

This framework is a reusable conceptual and research tool. It does not claim that every restriction is harmful, that every unconventional use is legitimate, or that adaptation is always technically, legally, or financially feasible. A complete analysis should identify the affected population, time period, actual rules, documented operational problems, alternatives, and consequences of both restriction and adaptation.
