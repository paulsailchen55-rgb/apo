# Archival Details

**Primary category:** Research question / framework-concept

**Status:** Early-stage research concept  
**Archive role:** Supporting research framework within the larger open-market computing project

## Origin

This concept emerged from discussion of the difficulty an ordinary person may face when trying to understand the legal and technical status of a modern computing device.

The starting observation was that a single device can carry multiple overlapping timelines: patents, copyrights, licenses, component rights, regulatory conditions, support periods, and other restrictions. These timelines may begin, renew, expire, or change at different times and may differ by jurisdiction.

The discussion connected this problem directly to the Open Market Computing Products proposal. A parallel market for genuinely open computing products would be difficult to exercise if the boundaries of permissible repair, modification, experimentation, and development were invisible.

## Core question

> If a machine has many clocks around it, how can an ordinary person see those clocks clearly enough to understand what they can legitimately do with the machine?

## Three related concepts

### 1. Intellectual Property Clocks

Different rights and restrictions can have different starting points, durations, renewal requirements, expiration dates, and jurisdictions.

### 2. Device Legal Status Map

A product-level representation that translates those underlying rights and restrictions into a human-readable description of what is permitted, restricted, conditional, jurisdiction-dependent, or uncertain.

### 3. Temporal Accountability

A standardized record of important dates and status changes affecting a device, including the object or component involved, jurisdiction, source of the status, and the event that changes the status.

## Relationship to the main project

This research does not propose eliminating intellectual property.

It asks whether an open-market computing category needs a visible boundary system so that a person can distinguish:

- what they own physically;
- what they are licensed to use;
- what they may repair;
- what they may modify;
- what they may study;
- what they may develop against;
- what remains restricted;
- what is jurisdiction-dependent;
- what changes over time.

The concept therefore supports the larger project's goal of creating a parallel market category rather than requiring every computing product to become open.

## Human-access principle

A recurring motivation is the possibility of a legitimate **"day of experimentation"** for an individual: a child, student, hobbyist, researcher, repairer, artist, teacher, independent developer, or ordinary owner who does not have access to corporate legal and engineering resources.

The proposed system should not promise that every action is lawful. Instead, it should make the relevant boundary visible enough that a person can understand when professional legal advice or additional permission is required.

## Jurisdictional principle

The archive should not assume that one universal clock exists.

Patent and other intellectual-property rights can be jurisdiction-specific, and international systems can involve national or regional stages. A useful status map therefore needs to preserve geographic distinctions rather than collapse them into one global expiration date.

## Proposed future artifact

A mature version of this research could define a Device Openness / Legal Clock Map with fields such as:

- device and component identity;
- relevant right or restriction;
- right holder or administrator;
- legal category;
- jurisdiction;
- beginning date;
- expiration or review date;
- renewal or maintenance event;
- continuation or extension status;
- applicable license;
- technical restriction;
- commercial support status;
- evidence/source;
- confidence or uncertainty;
- next significant event;
- resulting change in permitted activity.

## Evidence standard

Future factual claims about legal status should be tied to authoritative records where possible. The archive should distinguish:

1. documented legal facts;
2. manufacturer policies;
3. technical limitations;
4. contractual terms;
5. interpretation;
6. unresolved questions.

The clock map is therefore a transparency and navigation concept, not a substitute for legal advice.

## Archive boundary

This concept should remain distinct from:

- the Garmin-specific corporate proposal;
- the general corporate open-product proposal;
- U.S. and international policy proposals;
- the broader philosophical question of intellectual-property stewardship.

Those subjects may interact with the clock-map concept, but they should not be collapsed into it.

## Working principle

> **Make the clocks visible before asking the individual to navigate them.**

The intended contribution of this archive item is to turn an otherwise diffuse experience of legal and technological complexity into a concrete research question: whether the temporal structure of a computing product's restrictions can itself be represented as part of the product's accountability to its owner.
