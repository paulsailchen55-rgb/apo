# Existing-Home Materials Transition Pathway

**Status:** PROPOSED framework

## Purpose

Support people who want to retain an existing home while progressively improving its materials, systems, health characteristics, repairability, and end-of-life options.

This pathway applies to:

- Victorian houses
- Adobe and earthen buildings
- Historic homes
- Ordinary older homes
- Contemporary homes containing plastic-heavy or difficult-to-manage materials
- Homes undergoing repair, renovation, accessibility work, energy upgrades, or phased replacement

## Core Principle

Demolition is not the default starting point. The first question should be:

> What can be safely retained, repaired, removed, substituted, or redesigned in stages?

Retention must not be assumed to be automatically safer or more sustainable. Existing conditions, moisture, structural integrity, fire safety, indoor air quality, hazardous materials, code triggers, and lifecycle impacts require investigation.

## Relationship to the Dome/Yurt Pathways Project

This is a complementary pathway, not a requirement that every applicant build a dome or yurt.

The dome/yurt project can direct applicants here when they:

- Decide not to demolish an existing home
- Want to transition an existing home toward lower-plastic and more repairable materials
- Own or occupy a historic, Victorian, Adobe, or other culturally significant structure
- Need a phased plan because full replacement is financially or practically unrealistic

A future standalone project may expand this framework into a general **Existing Buildings Materials Transition** project for all building types.

## Materials and Omega Principle Screening

For each proposed intervention, record:

- Existing material and location
- Why it is being retained, repaired, removed, or replaced
- Plastic, petrochemical, resin, adhesive, coating, or composite content
- Exposure to occupants, moisture, heat, fire, and weather
- Repair and maintenance requirements
- Expected service life
- Ability to disassemble without destroying adjacent materials
- Reuse, recovery, recycling, biodegradation, or disposal options
- Availability of safer and technically suitable alternatives
- Labor, tools, skills, and local supply chain
- Structural, fire, seismic, accessibility, energy, and moisture implications
- Applicable permits and professional review

## Phased Intervention Sequence

1. Document the building and its history.
2. Identify urgent safety, structural, moisture, fire, health, and accessibility issues.
3. Inventory existing materials and systems.
4. Prevent unnecessary removal of serviceable materials.
5. Repair before replacing where feasible.
6. Prioritize reversible, maintainable, and separable interventions.
7. Replace problematic materials in planned stages when evidence supports doing so.
8. Record removed materials and their actual handling route.
9. Reassess the building after each phase.

## Historic and Adobe Buildings

Historic status, cultural significance, and original construction methods must be checked before alteration. California's Historical Building Code may provide alternative compliance methods for qualified historic buildings, but it does not eliminate the need for reasonable safety or jurisdictional review.

Adobe and other earthen buildings require specialized assessment because moisture management, seismic behavior, structural continuity, compatible repairs, and inappropriate coatings can materially affect performance.

## Evidence Rules

Do not label a material as natural, non-toxic, biodegradable, recyclable, fire-safe, seismic-safe, or code-compliant without evidence for the specific product, assembly, conditions, and jurisdiction.

Do not assume that removing plastic is beneficial if the replacement creates greater fire, moisture, structural, health, or lifecycle risks. Document tradeoffs openly.

## Future Research Questions

- Which materials commonly plasticize older California homes during renovation?
- Which interventions are reversible and which create composite assemblies that are difficult to separate?
- How can natural materials be introduced without damaging historic fabric or earthen walls?
- What local trades and suppliers can perform repair-oriented work?
- What permitting thresholds apply to phased material replacement?
- What practical end-of-life routes exist for removed materials today?
- How should Phoenix and Dragonfly concepts be recorded as future infrastructure rather than assumed present-day disposal solutions?

## Source Anchors

California State Parks' Office of Historic Preservation identifies adaptive reuse, retention, condition assessment, and sustainable rehabilitation as important preservation practices. CalRecycle identifies material selection factors including low toxicity, low emissions, durability, local production, recyclability, and reuse.

These sources support the research direction but do not approve any particular material or assembly.

## Limitations

This document is a planning framework, not engineering advice, historic-preservation approval, hazardous-materials guidance, or permit authorization.
