# Modular Parking Garage / Building Platform
## Santa Cruz and California — Weird Housing Infrastructure Project

**Project status:** PROPOSED / SPECULATIVE research concept  
**Purpose:** Preserve the Response 22 discussion as a standalone project document for the APO housing research archive.

---

## 1. The real weird project

The really weird project is not simply “housing policy.”

It is the **modular parking-garage / building-system concept** developed in the other projects, and then asking:

> **What would this physical building system mean if we treated it as a California housing-infrastructure experiment?**

That deserves its own branch of the state-level housing analysis.

---

## 2. The core idea

Think of the project as:

**MODULAR STRUCTURAL FRAME → BUILDING PLATFORM → DIFFERENT USES OVER TIME**

Rather than designing a conventional apartment building whose entire physical form is locked in from day one, the concept is a reconfigurable piece of infrastructure.

```text
                 MODULAR STRUCTURAL FRAME
                          │
          ┌───────────────┼───────────────┐
          ↓               ↓               ↓
       PARKING         HOUSING          WORKSPACE
          │               │               │
          └───────────────┼───────────────┘
                          ↓
                  COMMUNITY USE
                          ↓
                 FUTURE ADAPTATION
```

The unusual part is that **the parking structure is not necessarily the final building use.**

It becomes a **reconfigurable piece of infrastructure**.

That connects strongly to the housing transmission project.

---

## 3. Why this matters in California

California already recognizes parking as something that can affect housing feasibility.

HCD states that excessive parking standards can increase development costs and reduce land available for housing or amenities. HCD also requires Housing Elements to consider the cumulative effect of development standards. California's AB 2097 generally prohibits local agencies from imposing minimum automobile parking requirements on qualifying projects within one-half mile of a major transit stop, subject to statutory exceptions. [CURRENT STATE LAW / HCD SOURCE — VERIFY FOR A SPECIFIC PROJECT]

This project asks a different question from simply eliminating parking:

> **What if parking infrastructure itself became a reusable housing-development asset?**

That is different.

## 4. The building-system experiment

Imagine a structure designed from the beginning around:

- beams
- floor modules
- utility connections
- stairs/elevators
- fire/life-safety systems
- exterior-envelope attachment points

The initial configuration could be:

**PARKING**

But the structural grid would be investigated for whether later modules could become:

- apartments
- studios
- offices
- classrooms
- clinics
- community rooms
- workshops
- storage
- emergency housing
- mixed-use space

The research question is **not** whether every existing parking garage could do this.

It is:

> **Can a standardized structural platform reduce the amount of demolition, redesign, permitting, financing, and material waste required when a community's needs change?**

That is a **transmission-efficiency question**.

## 6. The weird economic question

A conventional building tends to assume:

> **USE → BUILDING**

This concept reverses that:

> **BUILDING PLATFORM → MANY POSSIBLE USES**

That could change the feasibility equation.

Instead of:

**construction cost → one use → eventual demolition**

we investigate:

**platform cost → multiple configurations → multiple revenue/use cycles → adaptation → longer useful life**

A proposed research model is:

> **Asset value = initial use + adaptability + avoided reconstruction + retained infrastructure + future-use capacity**

**Important:** This is a proposed research model, not an established appraisal or accounting formula.

## 7. California's modular-building system

This concept is not entirely outside California's regulatory vocabulary.

California HCD already has a **Factory-Built Housing Program** covering factory-constructed residential buildings and components. HCD describes building components as subsystems, subassemblies, or systems that can include structural, electrical, mechanical, plumbing, and fire-protection systems. citeturn0search0

California also regulates commercial modulars and special-purpose commercial modulars. The 2025 California Building Standards Code applies to relevant new plans, designs, quality-control manuals, units, projects, and developments commencing production after January 1, 2026. citeturn0search12

Therefore the state-level research question becomes:

> **Can modularity be used not merely to build faster, but to make the building itself adaptable across housing, parking, commercial, emergency, and community uses?**

That remains a hypothesis.

## 9. The central question

The central question should be:

> **Can a building be designed as infrastructure rather than as a single-purpose product?**

And the California version:

> **Could a standardized, modular, code-compliant building platform reduce the repeated regulatory, financial, material, and construction transactions required when California's housing and community needs change?**

That connects three project branches:

### Housing Transmission Audit

Where does housing capacity get lost?

### Outside-the-Stack / Weird Housing Lab

What alternative system could avoid those losses?

### Modular Building Platform

Can the physical asset itself be redesigned so the system needs fewer disruptive transactions?

## 10. Do not assume the modular garage solves the problem

The project should **not** begin with the conclusion that modular construction is superior.

It should be treated as a controlled research experiment:

**CONVENTIONAL BUILDING**

versus

**MODULAR / ADAPTABLE PLATFORM**

Then compare, where evidence can be obtained:

- initial cost
- construction time
- permitting interfaces
- design changes
- financing requirements

- utility connections
- parking requirements
- conversion cost
- maintenance
- useful life
- material reuse
- demolition avoided
- adaptability
- revenue/use cycles
- affordable housing output
- successful occupancy
- long-term housing stability

The objective is to determine whether the proposed platform actually reduces **friction, distortion, delay, or loss**.

## 12. Research questions

1. Can one structural platform legally and economically support multiple building uses over its useful life?
2. Which components should remain permanent?
3. Which components should be replaceable or reconfigurable?
4. Can utility infrastructure be designed for future changes of use?
5. What happens to permitting when the structural platform is standardized but the interior use changes?
6. Which changes constitute a new building project under California law?
7. Which approvals can be reused?
8. Which approvals must be repeated?
9. Can modularity reduce construction time without simply shifting complexity into design, transportation, foundations, or permitting?
10. Does adaptability have measurable economic value?
11. Does adaptability reduce long-term demolition and reconstruction?
12. Could a parking-first structure become housing without requiring the entire structure to be rebuilt?
13. What seismic constraints govern conversion?
14. What fire/life-safety constraints govern conversion?
15. How do accessibility requirements affect modular conversion?
16. How do parking rules affect the initial configuration?
17. How do impact fees apply when the building changes use?
18. How does financing treat an asset whose future use is intentionally flexible?
19. Can a building's useful life be extended enough to change its total lifecycle economics?
20. Does the platform produce more stable housing over its lifecycle than a single-purpose building?
21. Does the platform reduce administrative transaction cost?
22. Does it merely relocate administrative friction to another interface?
23. Can a standardized platform reduce repeated engineering?
24. Can a standardized platform reduce repeated procurement?
25. Can California create a regulatory pathway for repeatable, adaptable building platforms without weakening health, safety, accessibility, seismic, or fire protections?

## 13. Difficult question

The difficult question is not:

> “Can we build a modular parking garage?”

It is:

> **Why are we repeatedly treating buildings as disposable answers to temporary configurations of human need?**

If the need changes from:

**PARKING → HOUSING → COMMUNITY SPACE → EMERGENCY USE → HOUSING**

perhaps the infrastructure should be capable of changing with it.

That is the proposition to test, not the conclusion.

## 16. Initial state-level source basis

California HCD states that its Factory-Built Housing Program regulates the manufacturing of factory-built housing and building components in California and identifies structural, electrical, mechanical, plumbing, and fire-protection systems among possible building-component systems. citeturn0search0

HCD's current building-code information states that the 2025 California Building Standards Code became effective January 1, 2026, and includes requirements applicable to factory-built housing and commercial modulars. citeturn0search12turn0search9

HCD also states that California's housing-element framework addresses manufactured and factory-built housing and requires local jurisdictions to provide appropriate zoning and development opportunities for qualifying factory-built housing. citeturn0search6

These sources establish that modular/factory-built construction is already part of California's regulatory system. They **do not establish that the proposed adaptable parking-garage platform is feasible, economical, or legally approvable**. Those questions remain for the research.
