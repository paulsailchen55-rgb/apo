# 2. The first possible transmission loss: NEED → ALLOCATION

HCD's determination is not simply “how many houses should California build?”

The process considers demographic information and also existing conditions such as:

- overcrowding
- cost burden
- vacancy rates
- jobs/housing imbalance
- projected population and household growth
- certain housing losses from emergencies. ([California Housing Dept.](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/rhna/process?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Current Housing Planning Process | California Department of Housing and Community Development"))

Then the regional number gets allocated.

So we should distinguish:

**Housing need**

from

**Housing allocation**

from

**Housing capacity**

from

**Housing production**

from

**Housing occupancy**

from

**Housing stability**

Those are not the same measurement.

# 3. RHNA is a planning requirement, not the same thing as occupied housing

This is one of the most important distinctions for our project.

HCD describes RHNA as a **minimum projection of additional housing units needed** during the planning period. Local governments receive credit for qualifying units that are approved, permitted, or built, depending on the applicable rules and documentation. ([California Housing Dept.](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/housing-element/building-blocks/projected-rhna?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Projected Housing Needs - Regional Housing Needs Allocation | California Department of Housing and Community Development"))

That means we can construct an analytical ladder:

| LevelWhat is being measured? |                                    |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Need                         | Households/housing need identified |
| Allocation                   | Responsibility assigned            |
| Planning                     | Land/zoning identified             |
| Capacity                     | Units theoretically accommodated   |
| Entitlement                  | Projects approved                  |
| Construction                 | Units physically produced          |
| Lease-up                     | Units successfully occupied        |
| Affordability                | Household can afford/qualify       |
| Stability                    | Household remains housed           |

**Our project should not collapse these into one number.**

That may be one of the biggest findings of the state-level analysis.

This is where our **Technical Difficulties / Friction / Distortion / Loss** framework becomes particularly interesting.

HCD says Housing Elements must analyze:

> land-use controls, building codes and enforcement, site improvements, fees/exactions, and local processing and permit procedures.

And HCD specifically says the analysis should evaluate their **cumulative impact** on housing supply and affordability. ([California Housing Dept.](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/housing-element/building-blocks/land-use?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Land-Use Controls | California Department of Housing and Community Development"))

That is almost exactly the door we need.

# 5. State-level “friction” is already documented by HCD

This is particularly useful.

HCD identifies common permitting constraints including:

- lengthy processing
- unclear procedures
- layered reviews
- multiple discretionary reviews
- costly conditions of approval.

HCD says these can increase housing costs, uncertainty, and financial risk. ([California Housing Dept.](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/housing-element/building-blocks/processing-permitting-procedures?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Processing and Permitting Procedures | California Department of Housing and Community Development"))

So we can now distinguish:

### Policy objective

**Build safe, adequate, affordable housing.**

### Mechanism

**Regulation + planning + financing + infrastructure + affordability requirements.**

### Friction

**Time + documentation + reviews + fees + uncertainty + coordination.**

### Distortion

The project that emerges may differ from the housing opportunity originally envisioned.

### Loss

Some theoretically possible housing never reaches construction, occupancy, or long-term stability.

This is no longer merely our metaphor. **The state's own housing-element framework recognizes cumulative constraints and their effects.** ([California Housing Dept.](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/housing-element/building-blocks/land-use?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Land-Use Controls | California Department of Housing and Community Development"))

# 6. Fees are particularly interesting at the state level

HCD explicitly tells jurisdictions to examine **cumulative development fees** and their proportion of overall development costs.

It even suggests examining whether fees constitute a significant enough portion of development costs to indicate a constraint. HCD gives **10–15% of development costs** as a typical reference point, while noting that the appropriate percentage varies with land, construction, and other conditions. ([California Housing Dept.](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/housing-element/building-blocks/constraints-fees?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Fees and Exactions | California Department of Housing and Community Development"))

That's important because it gives us a possible quantitative experiment.

Instead of asking:

> “Is this fee good or bad?”

we ask:

> **At what point does cumulative fee/exaction burden materially change project feasibility?**

Then:

> **What happens to the housing output when that burden changes?**

And ultimately:

> **How many stable homes are produced per dollar of cumulative system cost?**

That's much more rigorous.

# 7. The state has another interesting requirement: “realistic development capacity”

HCD doesn't simply accept theoretical land capacity.

For certain sites, the Housing Element must demonstrate that lower-income housing development is **realistic or feasible**. HCD identifies evidence such as previous comparable development, developer interest, lot consolidation potential, appropriate density, and other evidence of feasibility. ([California Housing Dept.](https://www.hcd.ca.gov/housing-element/building-blocks/sites-zoning/development-capacity?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Realistic Development Capacity | California Department of Housing and Community Development"))

This is extremely relevant to our project.

Because it creates a distinction between:

**PAPER CAPACITY**

and

**REALISTIC CAPACITY**

That gives us another transmission test:

```text
ZONED CAPACITY
      ↓
REALISTIC DEVELOPMENT CAPACITY
      ↓
FINANCIALLY FEASIBLE CAPACITY
      ↓
ACTUAL DEVELOPMENT
      ↓
ACTUAL OCCUPANCY
```

Each arrow is an interface.

Each interface can be audited.
