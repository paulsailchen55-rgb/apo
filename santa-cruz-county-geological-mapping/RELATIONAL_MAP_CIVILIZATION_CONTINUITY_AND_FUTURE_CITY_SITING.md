# 1. Purpose of This Note

The geological mapping work should eventually produce more than a map of faults, rock units, landslides, scarps, elevations, or other geological features.

One possible long-term product is a **relational map for civilization continuity**:

> If the present arrangement of cities, infrastructure, institutions, and settlements were severely disrupted, where would the best physically informed places be for people to regroup, preserve knowledge, establish temporary settlements, and eventually rebuild durable cities?

This is not intended to be a prediction of where a disaster will occur, nor a declaration that any existing city is “wrong.” It is a planning framework for a different time horizon.

The central distinction is between:

1. **Emergency refuge locations** — places people can reach and use during or immediately after a disaster.
2. **Recovery locations** — places suitable for temporary or intermediate rebuilding.
3. **Long-term city locations** — places that may provide a durable physical foundation for settlement over generations.
4. **Civilization-preservation locations** — places that should be identified and protected in advance because they may be important options for future rebuilding even if they are not appropriate to develop now.

The fourth category is particularly important.

A place does not have to be developed today to be valuable. A location currently classified as parkland, open space, conservation land, difficult terrain, or otherwise undeveloped could contain a future settlement opportunity. The planning objective may therefore be to **identify and preserve options rather than immediately consume them**.

---

# 2. The Long-Term Question

The underlying question can be stated simply:

> **Where should civilization be capable of starting again?**

A more technical formulation is:

> Given the geological, geomorphic, hydrologic, climatic, ecological, transportation, resource, and human-history evidence available to a civilization, which locations provide comparatively durable foundations for settlement under a range of possible future conditions?

This is different from conventional city planning.

Conventional planning often starts from existing cities and asks:

- How do we protect this city?
- How do we repair this infrastructure?
- Where can additional housing go?
- Where should transportation be expanded?
- Where can economic activity be concentrated?

This framework asks a deeper question:

> **If we were not constrained by the locations inherited from the past, where would we choose to establish a durable settlement now?**

The inherited map remains important, but it is no longer the only possible map.

---

# 3. Civilization Should Not Be Assumed to Be Permanent

A basic premise of this framework is that civilization should not assume that its present configuration will continue indefinitely.

That does not require assuming a catastrophe.

It simply recognizes that:

- cities change;
- coastlines change;
- rivers change;
- mountains rise and erode;
- faults move;
- sediment accumulates or is removed;
- groundwater systems change;
- climate changes;
- infrastructure ages;
- political boundaries change;
- technologies change;
- economic systems change;
- populations move;
- institutions disappear and are replaced.

A civilization that survives for many generations should therefore leave **options for the people who come after it**.

The planning horizon should include not only the next election, budget, infrastructure cycle, decade, or century, but also the possibility that future people will need to reorganize settlement under circumstances we cannot presently predict.

The goal is not to predict their exact circumstances.

The goal is to make sure they have **good choices available**.

# 4. Two Different Maps: Emergency Safety and Future City Placement

The work already undertaken in the user's mapping has a distinction that should be preserved explicitly.

## 4.1 Emergency map

The first map asks:

> **Where can people go when something happens?**

This includes the user's existing work identifying relatively safer locations above the user's proposed “stadia line” in San Luis Obispo County and in northern and southern portions of Santa Cruz County.

The emergency map may eventually incorporate:

- elevation;
- flood exposure;
- tsunami exposure;
- landslide/debris-flow exposure;
- fault rupture;
- shaking intensity;
- liquefaction susceptibility;
- wildfire exposure;
- coastal erosion;
- storm exposure;
- road access;
- bridge dependence;
- water availability;
- communications;
- medical access;
- evacuation routes;
- shelter capacity;
- terrain accessibility.

This is a **response map**.

It asks where people may go when they cannot remain where they are.

## 4.2 Future-city map

The second map asks a different question:

> **After the immediate crisis is over, where should long-term settlement be rebuilt or established?**

This requires a much larger evidence set.

A location that is excellent for emergency refuge is not automatically a good city site.

A temporary refuge may prioritize:

- accessibility;
- immediate safety;
- elevation;
- open space;
- emergency transportation.

A permanent city additionally requires:

- stable geological substrate;
- long-term water security;
- manageable slope processes;
- durable transportation connections;
- food-production potential;
- ecological compatibility;
- manageable fire exposure;
- manageable flood exposure;
- acceptable earthquake consequences;
- adequate building materials;
- energy access;
- waste management;
- sanitation;
- long-term climatic suitability;
- room for adaptation;
- and the ability to remain useful as surrounding environmental conditions change.

The two maps should therefore remain separate while being relationally connected.

# 5. The “Worst-Case” Earthquake Scenario

One scenario motivating this framework is a very large earthquake sequence affecting the Pacific-margin urban system.

The user has referred to earthquakes in the **M7.5+ and M8+ range** as a conceptual extreme-disruption scenario.

This should be stored as a **planning scenario**, not as a claim that an earthquake of a particular magnitude would automatically destroy every city.

Earthquake consequences vary enormously according to:

- rupture location;
- rupture length;
- depth;
- distance from populated areas;
- directionality;
- duration;
- soil conditions;
- basin effects;
- building type;
- infrastructure age;
- bridges;
- utilities;
- fire following earthquake;
- landslides;
- liquefaction;
- tsunami;
- aftershocks;
- transportation failure;
- and the geographic distribution of damage.

The useful conceptual scenario is therefore:

> **What if the inherited urban system were damaged badly enough that rebuilding it exactly as it existed before was no longer the obvious or practical choice?**

That is the situation in which the future-city map becomes valuable.

The map would already exist before the event.

People would not have to invent a new geography while experiencing the disaster.

# 6. The “Empty Map” Thought Experiment

A powerful version of the exercise is to temporarily remove the existing cities from the map.

Do not erase them historically.

Instead, create a planning layer in which they are treated as absent.

Ask:

> **If the map contained geology, water, terrain, climate, ecological systems, transportation corridors, and human knowledge—but no inherited cities—where would we place cities?**

This is a counterfactual planning experiment.

It can reveal assumptions that are invisible when planning starts with existing urban boundaries.

Existing cities may have developed because of:

- ports;
- railroads;
- military considerations;
- defensive geography;
- access to mines;
- agriculture;
- rivers;
- industrial fuel;
- highways;
- historical land ownership;
- political boundaries;
- economic concentration;
- colonial settlement patterns;
- proximity to shipping;
- or the technologies available at the time.

Those reasons can remain historically legitimate without being sufficient reasons for future settlement.

The future-city map asks whether another generation, with different technology and different priorities, would choose the same locations.

# 8. Difficult Terrain Is Not Automatically Bad Terrain

A location can be difficult to build in without being unsuitable for civilization.

Historically, difficult terrain may have been avoided because it required:

- extensive road construction;
- difficult transportation;
- water lifting;
- grading;
- retaining structures;
- specialized foundations;
- energy-intensive construction;
- difficult agriculture;
- difficult communication.

Modern and future technology changes those constraints.

The question therefore should not be:

> “Is this terrain easy to build on?”

It should be:

> **“What does this terrain require, what processes act upon it, and can those requirements be met without creating a new long-term instability?”**

A steep location with stable bedrock, reliable water, favorable orientation, manageable wildfire exposure, and appropriate engineering could potentially have a different long-term value from a flat location built on deep unconsolidated sediment.

Conversely, a beautiful elevated location could still be unsuitable because of landslides, fault rupture, water scarcity, fire, access limitations, ecological constraints, or other hazards.

The map should preserve both possibilities.

# 9. Preserve Future Sites Before Developing Them

One of the strongest planning implications of this concept is:

> **A future city site does not have to become a city now.**

The mapping project could identify candidate locations and then establish a separate **Future Settlement Reserve / Civilization Continuity Reserve** concept.

Such a reserve could mean:

- do not assume development is required;
- do not grade the land prematurely;
- do not consume the site simply because it appears unused;
- preserve access for future evaluation;
- preserve geological evidence;
- preserve ecological evidence;
- preserve water systems;
- continue monitoring;
- periodically reevaluate suitability;
- document why the site was preserved;
- and allow future generations to decide whether and when the site should be used.

The purpose is not to predetermine the future.

It is to prevent the present from eliminating future choices.

A place can therefore have a planning value even when its current use is:

- parkland;
- open space;
- conservation land;
- agricultural land;
- forest;
- watershed;
- undeveloped terrain;
- or another nonurban use.

The map should not equate “not currently developed” with “available for development.”

Instead:

> **Some undeveloped places may be valuable precisely because they remain undeveloped.**

# 10. The “Stadia Line” as an Existing Observation/Research Layer

The user has already developed a concept called the **stadia line**.

For this archive, the term should be preserved exactly as a user-defined operational concept rather than silently converted into an established scientific definition.

The user's description is approximately:

> Begin with the present ocean surface as a practical reference and measure upward by one stadia. The resulting elevation is used as a line or threshold for examining terrain above that level.

The user has used this concept to map locations considered relatively safe or strategically useful in portions of San Luis Obispo County and Santa Cruz County.

The user also reports an observational pattern:

> Along portions of the mountains, a line of trees appears to occur near the elevation corresponding to the user's stadia reference, including locations farther inland.

The user interprets this as potentially related to a recurring behavior of water, moisture, fog, air, or another environmental phenomenon extending inland through valleys and mountain systems.

The user has also observed or believes that a related elevation relationship may extend through inland California toward Sacramento.

These observations are important to preserve.

They are **not yet established here as proof** of a particular atmospheric, hydrologic, fog, or ecological mechanism.

The appropriate archival treatment is:

- **Observation:** a recurring visual/geographic relationship has been perceived.
- **Operational layer:** the user's stadia elevation can be mapped.
- **Hypothesis:** environmental processes may correspond to or influence the observed elevation.
- **Evidence needed:** repeated measurements, meteorological observations, vegetation data, topography, remote sensing, water data, historical records, and independent field observations.
- **Alternative explanations:** vegetation may correlate with elevation for many reasons, including temperature, precipitation, soil, aspect, fire history, land use, species distribution, groundwater, fog, wind, or other factors.
- **Research status:** unresolved.

This preserves the observation without prematurely declaring the mechanism.

The user does **not** mean:

**Future Flood Level**

# 13. Base Level: The Problem of Finding a Baseline in a Moving Landscape

A central geological problem raised in this conversation is:

> **If both the land and the reference sea level are changing, what exactly is the baseline?**

This is a serious and useful question.

In geomorphology, sea level is often used as an important reference because rivers, shorelines, erosion, sedimentation, and coastal processes respond to base-level conditions.

But the land itself is not necessarily stationary.

The USGS explains that relative sea level reflects the combined effects of water-level change and vertical land motion. Land can rise or subside, meaning the apparent sea-level change at a location can differ from global ocean-volume change. citeturn0search0turn0search1

The same principle applies to a mountain.

If a mountain rises while erosion removes material, its present elevation is the result of competing processes.

If the ocean rises while the land rises, the relative relationship can change more slowly than either component alone.

If the land subsides while sea level rises, the relative change can be amplified.

Therefore:

> **A map of elevation is a snapshot of a moving system.**

# 14. Relative Versus Absolute Reference Frames

The project should explicitly distinguish several possible reference frames.

## 14.1 Present-day elevation

Elevation relative to a modern vertical datum.

Useful for:

- present engineering;
- emergency planning;
- current flood modeling;
- current infrastructure.

## 14.2 Relative sea level

The relationship between ocean surface and land surface at a particular location and time.

This includes:

- ocean-level change;
- tides;
- atmospheric effects;
- currents;
- tectonic uplift;
- tectonic subsidence;
- sediment compaction;
- groundwater-related subsidence;
- other vertical land motion.

## 14.3 Paleoelevation / paleo-sea level

A reconstructed relationship from geological evidence.

Evidence can include:

- marine terraces;
- shoreline angles;
- sediments;
- fossils;
- archaeological deposits;
- stratigraphic relationships;
- dating methods;
- geomorphic surfaces.

USGS work on the Pacific coast demonstrates that former sea-level indicators can occur at substantially different elevations because tectonic uplift varies geographically. citeturn0search6

## 14.4 Relative geomorphic position

Instead of asking only:

> “How high is this place?”

ask:

> “Where is this place relative to the active processes around it?”

Examples:

- above a floodplain;
- above a marine terrace;
- above a landslide toe;
- below a ridge crest;
- adjacent to a fault scarp;
- within a drainage basin;
- above a valley floor;
- below a sediment source.

This may ultimately be more useful for long-term settlement analysis than elevation alone.

# 15. Base Level as a Relational Variable

The concept of **base level** should therefore be treated as a relational variable, not simply a single number.

A working conceptual equation is:

**Relative surface position = land elevation − relevant base-level reference**

But both terms may vary with time.

A more useful conceptual form is:

**R(x,t) = Z_land(x,t) − Z_base(x,t)**

where:

- R(x,t) = relative position;
- Z_land(x,t) = land-surface elevation at location x and time t;
- Z_base(x,t) = relevant base-level reference at location x and time t.

This is not intended as a complete physical model.

It is a reminder that:

> **The baseline itself may move.**

For rivers, the relevant base level may be local or downstream rather than simply global mean sea level.

For coastal processes, the ocean surface and wave environment may be the important reference.

For groundwater, hydraulic head may matter.

For slope processes, local drainage position may matter.

For human settlement, the relevant reference may involve several interacting surfaces simultaneously.

# 16. Mountains as Moving Reference Surfaces

The user describes mountains as sometimes “growing” and sometimes “ungrowing,” including rising and sinking.

The scientific framework should preserve this in terms of competing processes:

- tectonic uplift;
- tectonic subsidence;
- fault displacement;
- folding;
- erosion;
- weathering;
- landsliding;
- sediment deposition;
- isostatic responses;
- volcanic construction where relevant.

USGS tectonic geomorphology research explicitly uses landforms and elevation measurements to study how mountains and fault zones deform over time. Fault-related landforms include scarps, folds, ridges, sag ponds, offset terraces, and displaced streams. citeturn0search7

Therefore the project should avoid treating current topography as a permanently fixed surface.

Instead:

> **Current topography is the present state of a process history.**

# 17. Sea Level as a Moving Reference Surface

The ocean also changes.

Relevant components include:

- tides;
- waves;
- storm surge;
- atmospheric pressure;
- wind;
- currents;
- thermal expansion;
- ice-volume changes;
- sediment movement;
- local vertical land motion.

USGS notes that sea level is not spatially identical everywhere and that relative sea-level measurements combine ocean and land movement. citeturn0search1

This means that a future-city map cannot simply say:

> “Everything above X meters is safe.”

Instead it should ask:

> **How does this location behave relative to changing water levels and changing land elevation?**

# 18. The Deep-Time Baseline Problem

There is an even deeper version of the question.

Suppose a marine terrace is now 50 meters above the ocean.

That does not necessarily mean:

> “The ocean was 50 meters higher.”

It could involve some combination of:

- former sea level;
- tectonic uplift;
- fault motion;
- erosion;
- deformation;
- local sedimentary processes.

This is why geomorphology and geochronology are important.

A present elevation can be interpreted only after asking what physical processes created it.

The existing project documents on stratigraphy, geomorphology, dendrochronology, lichenometry, LiDAR, and scarp diffusion should therefore be treated as supporting components of this future relational map.

# 19. A Future-City Map Should Be Time-Aware

The final map should not be static.

It should have a time dimension.

Potential layers could represent:

- present conditions;
- recent historical conditions;
- Holocene conditions;
- late Quaternary conditions;
- older geological conditions;
- projected future conditions;
- extreme-event scenarios.

The point is not to pretend that the past predicts the future exactly.

The point is to determine:

> **Which locations have repeatedly remained useful or relatively stable despite environmental change?**

That may be a more meaningful long-term question than simply asking which locations look safe today.

# 20. Stability Should Be Defined Relationally

“Stable land” should not initially mean:

> never moved.

Almost no natural landscape satisfies that definition over geological time.

Instead, potential stability might mean:

> **The location has maintained a relatively persistent physical function or favorable relationship with surrounding processes over the time scale relevant to the proposed human use.**

Examples might include:

- a surface that repeatedly remains above major flooding;
- a bedrock area that remains relatively intact while surrounding sediment moves;
- a terrace that persists while lower surfaces are reworked;
- a watershed position that remains hydrologically useful;
- a ridge or upland that remains accessible through multiple climatic states.

This definition is intentionally relational.

# 39. A Site Should Have an “Uncertainty Budget”

The project should preserve uncertainty explicitly.

For every major conclusion, record:

- what is known;
- what is inferred;
- what is observed;
- what is modeled;
- what is disputed;
- what is unknown.

For example:

**Known:** elevation from a specified DEM.

**Observed:** vegetation boundary appears near the user's stadia reference.

**Inferred:** moisture may influence vegetation distribution.

**Hypothesis:** atmospheric water transport may correspond to the observed elevation.

**Unknown:** whether the relationship persists across seasons, years, and counties.

This structure prevents the archive from losing the difference between an observation and an explanation.

# 41. “Persistent Ground” Rather Than “Safe Ground”

The existing research record already uses the idea of identifying physical ground conditions and geological processes.

For the future-city map, another useful term is:

> **persistent ground**

This does not mean motionless ground.

It means ground whose physical characteristics and position relative to important processes remain sufficiently persistent to support the intended human function over the selected time horizon.

This could eventually become a measurable research category.

# 42. The Longest-Term Question: What Survives Change?

Instead of asking only:

- geology;

A city should ideally not depend upon the assumption that the physical world will remain exactly as it is at the moment the city is built.

# 43. Settlement as an Adaptation to the Earth, Not the Reverse

A guiding principle for the future-city component is:

> **Place civilization where it can cooperate with the physical structure of the Earth rather than continuously fighting the Earth to remain in an inherited location.**

This does not mean abandoning engineering.

It means using engineering to work with:

- terrain;
- drainage;
- geology;
- water;
- ecology;
- natural transportation corridors;
- and long-term processes.

A city can be engineered.

But the engineering should not require pretending that geology, water, and climate are static.

# 44. Relationship to the Omega Principle

This concept can also connect conceptually with the user's broader “Omega principle” framework about thinking about the end of things.

The future-city map asks a civilization to think beyond:

- construction;
- maintenance;

A site should be evaluated not only for its usefulness at the beginning of a project, but for its possible usefulness at the end of that project's life.

# 45. The Core Preservation Principle

The most important planning principle to preserve from this conversation is:

> **Do not consume all of the future's choices in the present.**

A civilization should be capable of saying:

> “We do not need this place now. Therefore we will preserve the possibility that it may be important later.”

This is especially relevant to:

- open land;
- parks;
- watersheds;
- mountain terraces;
- geological reference sites;
- high-ground corridors;
- transportation corridors;
- emergency refuge areas;
- potential future city sites.

# 49. Final Working Principle

The long-term objective is not to produce a map that says:

> “Here is where humanity must live.”

It is to produce a map that says:

> **“Here is what we know about the Earth, here is how it changes, here are the places that may remain useful, here are the uncertainties, and here are the options we should preserve for the people who come after us.”**

The civilization-continuity map therefore becomes a form of **inheritance infrastructure**.

Its product is not only a city plan.

Its product is a preserved set of choices.

# 50. Short Working Statement

> **A civilization should map not only where it lives now, but where it could live again.**
>
> Existing cities are one layer of history, not the permanent boundary of possibility.
>
> Emergency refuge and permanent settlement are different questions.
>
> A place that is difficult to build on today may become viable with different technology.
>
> A place that is undeveloped today may be valuable precisely because it remains available to future generations.
>
> Mountains rise, erode, deform, and sometimes subside. Sea level changes, and relative sea level also changes as land moves. Therefore the baseline itself must be treated as a variable.
>
> The purpose of the relational map is not to predict every future event. It is to preserve enough geological, environmental, historical, and human evidence that future people can make better decisions after circumstances change.
>
> **Do not consume all of the future's choices in the present.**
>
> **Map the refuge. Map the recovery. Map the durable foundation. Preserve the options.**
