# Food, Body, and Environmental Exposure — Daily Field Observation

Author: Paul Statchen
Date: 2026-09-18
Context: Santa Cruz-area daily observation and food preparation
Status: Personal field note and research reflection; not a medical diagnosis, dietary prescription, or claim about what foods are safe for other people.

## Why this note is being preserved

This note records an unusually detailed day of observing the relationship among food preparation, season, surrounding vegetation, environmental exposure, bodily response, and decisions about what to consume.

The central observation is that eating can be treated as a relationship and an experiment in attention rather than simply accepting whatever food is placed in front of a person. The same principle may matter when thinking about food distribution, homelessness services, health, environmental exposure, and individual differences.

The meal was prepared in separate sections rather than mixing everything together at once.

Rice:

- The recalled price was approximately "six something an ounce or a pound"; the exact unit and price were not recorded.

## Environmental exposure and seasonal observation

The user described the current period as mugwort season, with grasses and other vegetation present, and reported already feeling triggered by the surrounding seasonal environment.

The user's concern is not simply about one food. They are observing possible interactions among:
- seasonal pollen/environmental exposure
- foods that may cross-react or otherwise feel reactive to the user
- the cumulative effect of multiple exposures
- the condition of the person's body at a particular time
- food preparation and cooking
- location and timing

These are personal observations and hypotheses. They should not be generalized into claims that a particular food is harmful to other people.

## The persimmon experiment

A persimmon found on the street became an especially clear example of the user's method of paying attention.

The food was encountered and presented as available, and the user initially picked it up. The user then:
1. considered it as food rather than automatically eating it;
2. tested it before eating;
3. cooked/prepared it;
4. waited and allowed time for the body to settle/wash out;
5. tested it again after cooking;
6. noticed that it still felt slightly triggering;
7. decided not to eat it and discarded it.

The user does not interpret this as simply wasting food. The discarded food provided information about the user's present condition and about what their body was communicating at that time.

The user also recognized an important limit: if a person repeatedly acquires food that they cannot use, an outside observer may reasonably describe the repeated pattern as wasteful. The user's distinction is between careless waste and a deliberate experiment in which a person notices a warning, learns from it, and stops consuming something that appears not to agree with them.

## Body awareness as a form of information gathering

The user's larger experiment is to become more aware of what the body is absorbing from food and the environment.

The user described this as becoming "hyper-aware" of:
- foods
- environmental pollen
- possible pollutants
- substances encountered in the surrounding environment
- bodily reactions
- the timing and sequence of exposures
- what happens after consumption or exposure

The purpose, as described by the user, is personal care and learning rather than proving a universal food rule.

This creates a useful distinction between:
- a food being objectively dangerous;
- a food being unsuitable for a particular person at a particular time;
- a person not yet knowing why they are reacting;
- a person learning through careful observation;
- a person being told what to eat without being given a way to understand their own response.

## Food access, homelessness, and relationship-based care

The user connected this observation to a broader question about food distribution to people experiencing homelessness or poverty.

The user's concern is that simply handing someone whatever food is available may not account for:
- individual dietary needs;
- allergies or sensitivities;
- existing environmental exposures;
- medication or health context;
- cultural or personal food practices;
- the person's ability to recognize and communicate bodily responses.

The user's proposed principle is relationship-based care: rather than assuming that one food package works for everyone, services should have ways of learning what works for each person and of allowing people to notice and communicate their own responses.

The user specifically emphasized that "dietary choices" can sometimes be misleading language when the real situation is limited options. A person may appear to choose from available foods while having very little actual choice.

The user also connected repeated exposure to foods or environmental conditions that do not agree with a person to a possible cycle in which the person seeks other substances or medicines to feel better. This is presented as a research question and personal interpretation, not as a demonstrated explanation of addiction or mental illness.

## Individual difference and non-judgment

The user emphasized that the same food may affect different bodies differently.

The user's example is that some people may have grown up in different environments or have different bodily conditions, exposures, or histories. Therefore, the observation that one person reacts to something should not become a basis for judging another person.

The proposed underlying principle is awareness without judgment:
- notice what is consumed;
- notice what happens afterward;
- learn from the response;
- avoid assuming another person's body works the same way.

## Spiritual and biblical reflection

The user connected this practice of attention to biblical and spiritual teachings.

The user recalled Leviticus as containing many rules concerning food and interpreted at least part of that tradition as training in awareness of what one consumes, rather than simply as a basis for judging other people.

The user also connected the reflection to Jesus' teaching about what enters a person and what comes out of a person, emphasizing output, speech, and results. The user's interpretation is that the significance of consumption can be considered through its resulting effects and what a person produces afterward.

These are the user's theological interpretations, not presented here as settled biblical scholarship. Exact scriptural wording and references should be verified separately before being quoted publicly.

The user also related the idea to the biblical image of people being watched or tested to determine whom they listen to. The user connected this with the contemporary possibility of tracking what people read, watch, purchase, or respond to in order to infer how they might behave or be influenced as consumers.

That contemporary comparison is recorded as the user's interpretation and concern. It should not be treated as proof of a particular surveillance system or hidden intention.

## "Watcher" concern and human agency

A central concern in the user's reflection is the difference between helping someone become more aware of themselves and having other people constantly watching them in order to classify, predict, control, or sell to them.

The user does not object to people attempting to understand consumer behavior as a general fact of commerce. The concern is what happens when observation of a person becomes a mechanism for presumed control.

The user's preferred direction is self-awareness:
- the person learns to notice their own body;
- the person learns what affects them;
- the person can communicate what they need;
- caregivers learn through relationship rather than surveillance;
- data, when collected, should not replace the person's own agency.

## Expired coffee found during environmental cleanup

The user also found a bag of coffee during the day's cleanup activity. The coffee had an expiration date in 2025 and was found well after that date in 2026.

The user considered whether expired coffee might have a use for plants or soil, but did not assume that it was healthy or appropriate to add directly to soil. The user recognized that plant/soil effects depend on the particular material, soil conditions, and plant conditions.

This is preserved as an open materials/soil question, not a recommendation to use expired coffee as fertilizer or soil amendment.

## Broader conceptual connection

The day's observations connect several previously separate lines of inquiry:

Food is an input.
The body is an observing system.
The environment is another source of inputs.
Preparation changes an input.
Timing changes context.
A bodily response is information.
A decision not to consume something can be a meaningful outcome rather than merely a failure to use available food.
Repeated observation can reveal personal patterns.
But personal patterns should not automatically become rules imposed on other people.

This suggests a broader research framework:

**Input -> preparation/exposure -> bodily response -> observation -> decision -> outcome -> learning**

The same framework can be considered for environmental exposure, housing conditions, lendable health/environment measurement, and relationship-based support services.
