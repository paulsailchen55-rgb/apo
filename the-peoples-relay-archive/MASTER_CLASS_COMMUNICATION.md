# Communication — Before There Were Words

## Working master-class concept

This is a proposed teaching framework, not a completed academic curriculum. Its purpose is to examine communication as human and biological infrastructure before treating it as a property of language, computers, or platforms.

## 1. Begin with an event

Something happens.

A being detects it.

The event becomes important.

The being responds.

Another being detects the response.

That sequence provides a simple starting point for asking what communication is without assuming that symbolic language came first.

Possible examples include vocalizations, gestures, visual signals, alarm calls, touch, smoke, marks, writing, messengers, telegraphy, radio, telephony, computers, networks, and AI-mediated communication.

### Important distinction

An observation about mammalian distress, bonding, birth, or danger signals is **not by itself proof of the historical origin of human language**. The idea that women specifically invented language from panic is retained here as the user's hypothesis/interpretive thesis, not as an established anthropological conclusion.

The stronger research question is:

> **What biological and social forms of communication existed before deliberate symbolic language, and what human needs did later communication systems inherit from them?**

## 2. Attention before representation

A working sequence for investigation is:

**existence → sensation → observation → significance → response → communication → representation → language → information systems**

This is a conceptual model, not a settled scientific sequence.

The master class can ask what happens when a system becomes so full of representations, interfaces, metrics, and processing that the original signal becomes difficult to perceive.

## 3. Panic as a revealing case

Panic is useful as an example of urgent communication, without claiming that panic is the sole origin of language.

A warning can be extremely small:

- Danger.
- Come here.
- I'm here.
- Where are you?
- I need you.
- It's safe.
- I found something.
- I love you.

The point is that communication can carry relationship, presence, distress, reassurance, intention, and attachment as well as factual propositions.

## 4. From signal to information theory

Once communication is treated as a channel between beings, familiar technical problems become human questions:

- noise
- distance
- delay
- loss
- ambiguity
- bandwidth
- encoding
- decoding
- redundancy
- error
- confirmation
- availability

Nyquist's work and later information theory can then be introduced as mathematical and engineering attempts to understand reliable transmission through a channel. The class should verify the historical development rather than retroactively assigning modern information theory to earlier communication practices.

A useful question is:

> **How much information can we reliably move through a channel, under the conditions that actually exist?**

## 5. The communication chain

A practical model is:

**Signal → Hearing → Interpretation → Understanding → Response**

Communication can fail at any stage.

A sender may know what they intended, while a receiver truthfully reports hearing something different. Neither statement necessarily proves deception. The channel, context, assumptions, or interpretation may have changed the result.

A recurring question for the master class is:

> **What did you hear?**

rather than only:

> **What did I say?**

## 6. The historical progression to investigate

A possible narrative spine is:

**human need → signal → language → writing → messenger → postal system → telegraph → telephone → radio → computer → Internet → mobile phone → AI**

This is a teaching sequence, not a claim that communication evolved in a single linear path. Each transition should be researched historically.

The central question is:

> **At what point, and through what mechanisms, can infrastructure surrounding communication become more concerned with its own institutional objectives than with the human purpose of the communication?**

## 7. When communication becomes machinery

The critique is not that corporations, governments, money, computers, or technology are inherently evil. The research question is what happens when the incentives of the institution carrying communication differ from the interests of the people communicating.

Possible topics include:

- advertising
- tracking
- behavioral profiling
- engagement optimization
- algorithmic amplification
- spam
- bot detection
- authentication requirements
- platform lock-in
- data extraction
- surveillance
- information overload
- unreliable availability

A useful distinction is:

> **Data about behavior is not the same thing as understanding intention.**

Analytics can record actions under particular technical and economic conditions. They do not, by themselves, establish the totality of a person's values or intentions.

## 8. Availability as part of communication

A message that cannot reliably reach its intended recipient is not a successful communication merely because a sender pressed a button.

A communications system can fail through:

**noise → delay → ambiguity → distortion → interruption → uncertainty**

The Relay concept therefore treats honest delivery state as important. Instead of one vague “sent” state, a system might distinguish:

**Prepared → Authorized → Accepted by Relay → Queued → Transmitted → Acknowledged**

If confirmation is unavailable, the system should say so rather than pretending success.

## 9. The early-public-computer lesson

The originating discussion includes a personal recollection of early public-library computers using older Windows/Internet Explorer-era software. The recollection is that public deployment went through years of instability, including websites/content that could overwhelm or crash systems and require IT intervention.

This is preserved as firsthand experience and a development lesson, not as a verified general history of the period.

The lesson is:

> **Every new communication technology has a history of intended behavior and a history of what actually happened when real people used it.**

Therefore:

> **Design for surprise.**

Test, observe, recover, fix, and test again.

## 10. The money question

A central economic question is:

> **What happens to communication when the institution carrying the communication has an economic incentive that differs from the communicator's purpose?**

Money is not itself the conclusion. Communication infrastructure can legitimately require resources: electricity, labor, hardware, software, maintenance, and support.

The design question is whether the financing mechanism causes the system to extract additional information, attention, or control from the communicator beyond what is reasonably necessary for the legitimate purpose.

This connects to the working principle of **non-extraction**.

## 11. Final question

The class can repeatedly return to one question:

> **What is communication for?**

Not merely what can a platform collect, optimize, amplify, monetize, or automate, but:

> **What does one being need to communicate to another being, and what infrastructure is necessary to make that communication reliable?**

A possible closing question is:

> **Did the thing that mattered actually make it from one being to another?**

## 12. Spiritual reflection

The originating conversation also treats communication in a spiritual frame: people and things may be understood as having a mission or purpose, and communication may be part of completing that purpose. References to God, praise, “Hallelujah,” death, inheritance, and the eventual return of things to “dust” belong to that personal/philosophical layer.

Those reflections should remain clearly distinguished from scientific claims. The class can hold both without pretending that one proves the other.

**COMMUNICATION: BEFORE THERE WERE WORDS**

*Observation, panic, love, information, machines, and the long journey from one nervous system to another.*
