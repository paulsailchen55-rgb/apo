# Santa Cruz Modular Public Infrastructure

## Archive status

This folder preserves a conversation-derived concept. It is **not** a completed project, engineering plan, legal determination, or established policy.

The working idea is to explore a distributed, reusable infrastructure model for Santa Cruz County/City: lease small portions of existing private or public space and deploy removable modular facilities rather than permanently converting or acquiring every property needed for a service.

## Core concept

The proposed system would combine:

- **Micro-leasing:** lease a defined portion of an otherwise usable property—such as parking spaces, a portion of a vacant building, or other suitable space—while leaving the remainder available to the owner or ordinary use.
- **Transferable modular infrastructure:** use units that can be installed, removed, relocated, recombined, and adapted as needs and locations change.
- **Distributed sites:** use multiple appropriately scaled locations rather than assuming one permanent facility must serve the entire community.
- **Shared-use access:** where feasible, make basic services available to a broad public rather than defining every facility solely around one population category.
- **Reversible intervention:** prefer installations that minimize permanent alteration of the underlying property, particularly where preservation, spiritual, cultural, neighborhood, or future-use considerations matter.
- **Public/private alignment:** allow property owners to earn lease income while retaining ownership and use of the larger property.

## Possible Santa Cruz applications discussed

The conversation focused on an area around Soquel Avenue/Dakota Avenue and San Lorenzo Park, plus potential downtown locations near public garages. Specific properties mentioned in the conversation include the former Good Times location and the Taj Mahal property. These are **ideas for investigation, not assertions that the properties are available, appropriate, or legally eligible for the proposed uses**.

A possible local configuration discussed was:

1. A community kitchen using or adapting an existing restaurant kitchen, potentially operating on a bring-your-own-groceries model with supervised cooking, basic food preparation, sanitation, and boiling-water facilities.
2. A public hygiene facility with showers, changing/rest areas, and basic secure storage.
3. Appropriate animal-support/storage arrangements where legally and operationally feasible.
4. Modular installations that could instead be placed in leased parking areas, vacant buildings, or other suitable spaces elsewhere in the city/county.
5. Downtown indoor space near public garages as another possible node in the network.
6. A modular installation associated with San Lorenzo Park or a soccer-park/ocean-side location, subject to site, park-use, environmental, accessibility, safety, and legal review.

The conversation also discussed the possibility of relocating or leasing replacement downtown space for existing businesses if a particular property were ever selected for public use. That is a **negotiation concept**, not a claim that any business should be displaced or that any particular property should be acquired.

## Proportionality principle

A central design principle developed in the conversation is **proportionality**:

> Use the smallest durable intervention that can accomplish the public purpose while preserving the greatest practical amount of flexibility for property owners, surrounding communities, existing businesses, and future uses.

Applied to property and infrastructure, this means asking:

- Does the City need to buy the entire property, or can it lease a small portion?
- Does the City need a permanent building conversion, or would a removable module work?
- Does one large facility need to serve everyone, or can services be distributed among smaller sites?
- Can an owner retain ordinary use of the majority of a property while leasing a defined portion?
- Can a facility be removed when the lease ends without leaving the property permanently altered?
- Can public-service space and revenue-generating uses coexist without one unnecessarily eliminating the other?

## Civic Space Exchange concept

A further idea is a city/county-managed **Civic Space Exchange**: a system through which owners could offer suitable temporary or partial-use space and public agencies or qualified service providers could match that space to modular infrastructure needs.

Potential listings could include:

- parking spaces
- portions of parking lots or garages
- vacant storefronts
- unused portions of commercial buildings
- warehouses
- vacant parcels
- community rooms
- seasonal or time-limited space

A listing could specify availability, area, access hours, permitted uses, maximum term, restrictions, utilities, accessibility characteristics, and other relevant conditions.

The concept resembles aspects of peer-to-peer space marketplaces, but the proposed civic system would need its own governance, contracting, insurance, accessibility, safety, privacy, procurement, and public-benefit rules. Existing commercial platforms or APIs could potentially be considered as integrations, but no specific integration is established by this archive.

## Property-owner proposition to test

The concept depends on evidence that micro-leasing would actually be attractive to property owners. That evidence should be researched rather than assumed.

Questions for a future feasibility study include:

- What revenue can owners obtain from currently underused parking or building space?
- How short can a useful lease be while remaining economically worthwhile?
- What percentage of a property can be leased without interfering with the owner's ordinary use?
- What insurance, indemnification, maintenance, utility, and security arrangements would owners require?
- What tax consequences would different lease structures have?
- Can owners preserve the property's long-term purpose while earning temporary income?
- Can reversible installations address concerns of owners who intentionally preserve property for cultural, spiritual, historical, ecological, or future-use reasons?
- What transaction costs would make micro-leasing impractical, and how could a standardized City/County agreement reduce them?

## Economic and operational hypothesis

The working hypothesis is that a reusable infrastructure asset may provide more location flexibility than a permanently dedicated building. Instead of treating real estate and service infrastructure as one inseparable asset, the City could potentially separate them:

**property lease → modular infrastructure → service operation**

Under this model, the underlying property can remain privately owned or retain its existing public purpose, while the infrastructure can move between sites.

This is a hypothesis to test with actual costs, legal analysis, operating data, and comparable examples.

## Housing Matters discussion: provenance and caution

The conversation included criticism of Housing Matters and of permanent or large-scale asset-focused approaches to public services. Those statements are preserved here as **the speaker's perspective and experience**, not as verified findings about Housing Matters, its finances, construction quality, intentions, or outcomes.

The policy question that can be separated from that criticism is:

> Could Santa Cruz complement permanent housing and service assets with a distributed, lease-based, transferable infrastructure layer?

That question can be researched independently of any judgment about a particular organization.

## Building and historic-preservation questions

The conversation also raised concerns about modern construction, remodeling, building-code requirements, fire-code requirements, indoor environmental quality, and historic-building preservation. These concerns should remain **research questions unless supported by project-specific evidence**.

Future work should compare, using documented evidence:

- new construction
- adaptive reuse
- modular construction
- removable/reversible installations
- lifecycle cost
- embodied carbon
- maintenance requirements
- indoor environmental quality
- fire and life safety
- seismic requirements
- accessibility
- historic-preservation constraints
- permitting timelines

The archive does not establish that newer buildings are inherently unsafe or that historic buildings are inherently safe. Those are questions requiring building-specific evidence.

## System-friction principle

Another principle developed in the conversation is that public systems should minimize unnecessary administrative friction.

A person navigating housing, hygiene, benefits, transportation, health, employment, courts, or municipal services may encounter many independent eligibility rules, forms, appointments, deadlines, offices, and documentation requirements. The cumulative burden can become substantial even when individual requirements have a legitimate purpose.

The proposed infrastructure model therefore pairs physical flexibility with an institutional goal:

> Reduce unnecessary friction, centralize what can be standardized, and preserve local flexibility where conditions genuinely differ.

## Research agenda

Before presenting this as a formal policy proposal, investigate:

1. Santa Cruz City and County property inventory and leasing authorities.
2. Current public hygiene and day-service needs and capacity.
3. California zoning, building, fire, health, accessibility, park-use, and historic-preservation requirements relevant to modular installations.
4. Public procurement and contracting requirements.
5. Insurance and liability models for temporary public facilities.
6. Existing modular shower, restroom, kitchen, laundry, storage, charging, and animal-support systems.
7. Comparable temporary-use or micro-lease programs in other cities.
8. Actual property-owner economics and lease pricing.
9. A pilot-site cost model comparing lease-plus-module against permanent acquisition/construction.
10. Governance and data requirements for a Civic Space Exchange.
11. Criteria for selecting, rotating, and retiring sites.
12. Community-engagement methods for surrounding residents and businesses.
