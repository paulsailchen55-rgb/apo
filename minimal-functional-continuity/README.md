# Minimal Functional Continuity

## Status
Concept / research / pilot framework. Not an engineering specification, legal standard, or adopted policy.

## Core idea
Minimal Functional Continuity is a proposed middle layer between normal utility service and total loss of functionality during an extended outage. The objective is to preserve a small but useful baseline of human function: communication, information access, lighting, charging, food preparation, water heating/boiling, and individually identified essential loads.

The objective comes before the technology. Solar, wind, storage chemistry, earth batteries, controllers, or future harvesting methods are means to evaluate rather than definitions of resilience.

## Key principles
- Minimum, not extreme normalcy.
- Continuity is the objective; technology is the means.
- Resilience should be distributed rather than dependent on unusually wealthy or highly prepared individuals.
- Use a shared building layer plus an individual resident layer.
- Intermittent continuity can be useful; every load need not run continuously.
- Measure actual loads before sizing storage.
- Efficiency comes before capacity.
- Storage should receive explicit safety, certification, propagation, lifecycle, service, repair, end-of-life and liability review.
- Do not transfer hidden risk from one part of the community to another.
- Regional manufacturing/service/repair/recycling may be a preference where technically and economically appropriate, not an absolute sourcing rule.
- Subsidy eligibility should not silently determine appropriate technology.
- Resilience should not depend on wealth or ownership status.
- Rental, lease, HOA and property constraints are part of resilience design.
- Future housing can potentially inherit a basic continuity layer rather than requiring every resident to retrofit it.
- Pilot before mandate.
- Ask the question before making the demand; route each question to the authority that can answer it.
- Do not require the future to remain loyal to today's solution.

## Demonstration building
A roughly 16-person residential building was used as a planning example. The architecture includes shared generation/storage and individual self-contained power units sized from measured essential loads.

Potential shared functions include emergency cooking, water heating, common lighting, communications and charging. Individual units can support resident-specific needs without forcing every apartment into one identical allowance.

## Individual load method
Use: device watts x hours/day = Wh/day. Then estimate candidate usable storage from daily energy, outage duration, conversion losses and reserve.

The user's AirDoctor nameplate was 95 W; a basic box fan was treated only as a provisional planning range until measured. These are example observations/planning assumptions, not universal device values.

Possible tiers: communications and lighting; communications plus fan; higher continuous-load equipment; unusually high-energy essential equipment requiring individual evaluation.

## Energy architecture
Solar is the proposed primary generation source for a pilot, with small wind as a possible supplemental source if site conditions justify it. A/B storage banks were discussed so one bank can serve while another charges or remains in reserve.

Earth/rod batteries remain an experimental low-power supplemental source. They should be treated as electrochemical energy sources, not ordinary electrical grounding. Their output must be measured at the actual site before assigning them a meaningful role.

Future rain, vibration, piezoelectric, waste-heat and other site-specific harvesting remains a separate research direction.

## Food and water continuity
Electrical continuity is only part of functional continuity. Food storage, cooking, water and lighting require separate capacity calculations and operating plans. A reserve only works if people can actually use and rotate it. Two medium communal cookers may provide useful redundancy compared with one oversized cooker, but this is a design hypothesis requiring testing.

## Safety and technology neutrality
The archive does not conclude that any battery chemistry is universally good or bad. LFP is still lithium-ion; chemistry alone is not a complete safety analysis. Evaluation should include failure behavior, thermal/fire characteristics, hazardous release, propagation, enclosure, certification, service life, repairability, recycling, supply chain, insurance and lifecycle cost.

## Housing and planning question
Can future multifamily residences be designed with a basic continuity layer so renters and future occupants do not have to independently purchase or permanently install resilience equipment? Building-owned, self-contained equipment may sometimes reduce lease/property barriers, subject to legal, fire, electrical, accessibility and property-management review.
