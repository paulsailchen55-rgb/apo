# Knowledge Is Not Ours Forever

I want to ask a question about intellectual property that I do not pretend to have completely answered.

Much of the modern economy places enormous value on intellectual property: patents, copyrights, trademarks, software, designs, scientific discoveries, processes, data, and other forms of knowledge. The United States Patent and Trademark Office now measures IP-intensive industries as representing an enormous portion of the American economy. (https://www.uspto.gov/ip-policy/economic-research/intellectual-property-and-us-economy)

I am not opposed to invention. I am not opposed to people receiving recognition, protection, or compensation for work they have done.

My question is deeper.

**Can knowledge ever truly belong to us in the same way that we imagine a physical possession belongs to us?**

A person may discover something in the world. Another person may understand it. Another may give it a name. Another may build upon it. Another may teach it to a child.

At every stage, something has passed through human beings.

And perhaps that matters.

The land was here before us. The oceans were here before us. The physical world was here before us. The capacity of human beings to observe, understand, imagine, and communicate was also not something any individual human being created from nothing.

We participate in it.

So perhaps intellectual property should sometimes be understood not only as **property**, but also as a **temporary stewardship of knowledge**.

That does not require eliminating intellectual property.

It asks whether there should always remain a pathway by which knowledge can return to the community.

A patent expires. A copyright eventually reaches its legal limits. Scientific knowledge can be published. Open licenses can permit people to use and build upon works. The idea of a knowledge commons already exists, and UNESCO has described knowledge commons and open science as ways of making knowledge available for broader human benefit. (https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/knowledge-commons-and-enclosures)

But I want to ask an even more basic question:

**What happens when we try to keep knowledge permanently enclosed?**

Food provides a simple metaphor.

Food can nourish us. But food is meant to pass through us. If we refuse that natural process and attempt to keep everything inside ourselves, what nourishes us can eventually become harmful.

Could knowledge work in a similar way?

Perhaps knowledge that is received, accumulated, and never allowed to circulate can become something other than nourishment.

It can become control.

It can become dependency.

It can become a barrier between people and the world they are trying to understand.

It can even become dangerous when access to essential knowledge is restricted indefinitely.

I am not saying that every discovery must immediately become free.

I am asking whether **permanent enclosure should ever be the highest purpose of knowledge.**

A corporation may hold knowledge.

A government may hold knowledge.

A university may hold knowledge.

An individual may hold knowledge.

But perhaps none of us should confuse holding something with having created the universe from which it became possible.

The ocean is an especially important place to ask this question.

If we learn something from the ocean, who owns that knowledge?

If we learn something from the land, who owns that knowledge?

If a community discovers something about the place where it lives, can that understanding eventually be locked away so that the community itself must pay to receive it back?

And if knowledge about the natural world becomes increasingly valuable, do we risk turning the world itself into an enormous collection of things that are increasingly inaccessible to the people who live within it?

I do not want to decide whether the source of this knowledge should be called God, nature, creation, the universe, evolution, collective human intelligence, or something else.

The name is not the point.

The point is the relationship.

**Perhaps knowledge passes through us more than it belongs to us.**

If that is true, then our responsibility may not be merely to extract value from knowledge.

It may also be to keep knowledge moving.

To teach it.

To share it.

To improve it.

To acknowledge those who contributed to it.

To protect legitimate creative work.

And eventually, when appropriate, to return what we have learned to the larger community from which future understanding can grow.

I am not asking for an answer here.

I am asking that we keep the question alive:

**If knowledge comes from a world that none of us created, how much of that knowledge should any of us be permitted to permanently enclose?**

Perhaps the measure of a civilization is not only how much knowledge it can acquire.

Perhaps it is also how well it allows knowledge to pass through it without becoming poison.
