# Open Market Computing Products

## Status

Potential project / policy research concept.

## Core concept

Investigate whether a healthy competitive computing market should preserve a parallel category of genuinely open consumer computing products alongside ordinary proprietary products.

The proposal does **not** ask corporations to make all of their products open source, abandon intellectual-property protection, or disclose everything they consider proprietary. It asks whether a manufacturer could continue selling closed, fully integrated products while also offering at least one deliberately open product or product line in a computing category.

An open product could be designed so that its owner and independent developers have meaningful legal and technical ability to modify, repair, study, and develop the hardware and software. The exact definition of "open" remains a research question.

## Parallel-market principle

Two categories can coexist:

- **Closed commercial product:** the manufacturer controls the integrated software and protects proprietary technology.
- **Open-market product:** the manufacturer sells a platform intentionally designed for independent modification and development, with clearly defined hardware and software openness.

The open product does not have to replace the proprietary product. It creates an additional market for developers, researchers, students, makers, accessibility developers, artists, small companies, startups, and people who want to experiment with computing systems.

The proposed policy question is whether governments should preserve or encourage this parallel market rather than requiring every product to follow either a completely proprietary or completely open model.

## Garmin as a first case study

A concrete corporate test case is a Garmin open-platform watch.

The proposal is not to make Garmin's existing watch ecosystem open. It is to ask whether Garmin could take an existing successful hardware platform, or a closely related platform, and produce a developer/open-hardware edition using substantially the same physical hardware.

The open edition could have:

- documented hardware interfaces;
- meaningful access to sensors and peripherals;
- a defined firmware/developer entry point;
- appropriate driver and interface documentation;
- an open reference software environment;
- a legally supported path for independent firmware and software development.

Garmin could continue protecting its ordinary proprietary firmware, services, algorithms, patents, industrial designs, manufacturing processes, and other commercially sensitive technology.

The fundamental question is:

> Could a company keep its proprietary product ecosystem while deliberately selling one product that is designed to be developed on?

## Corporate proposal

The corporate side of this project should eventually produce proposals addressed to individual manufacturers.

The first example is Garmin:

> "You already make excellent closed, integrated products. Would you consider creating one officially supported open hardware/software product, using the quality and hardware engineering of an existing commercial platform, for people who want to develop and experiment with what the machine can become?"

This is a platform proposal, not a request that the corporation adopt any particular application or invention.

## Public-policy proposal

The broader policy question is:

> Does a healthy open market in consumer computing require meaningful availability of products that owners are legally and technically permitted to modify, repair, study, and develop for?

The project should investigate whether governments can preserve or encourage such a market without eliminating legitimate intellectual-property protection or requiring disclosure of genuinely sensitive technology.

Possible policy mechanisms include:

- voluntary standards;
- procurement preferences;
- tax or other incentives;
- interoperability requirements;
- repairability requirements;
- owner rights concerning firmware and software;
- minimum documentation requirements;
- protected developer interfaces;
- exemptions from technological restrictions for lawful modification and research;
- requirements or incentives for manufacturers to offer at least one qualifying open product in particular computing categories.

The project should not assume that any one mechanism is correct.

## International scope

The concept is not inherently U.S.-specific.

Any country could investigate whether its own laws, competition policy, consumer-protection rules, repair laws, intellectual-property system, education policy, and technology standards leave enough room for an open consumer-computing market.

The United States is an initial focus because it provides a concrete environment for studying the FTC, DOJ Antitrust Division, U.S. Copyright Office / DMCA framework, Congress, FCC, NIST, state right-to-repair laws, and related institutions.

Other jurisdictions should be studied comparatively rather than treated as copies of the U.S. model.

## Research directions

Future work should examine:

1. Right to repair.
2. Interoperability.
3. Open-source hardware.
4. Firmware and bootloader access.
5. Consumer ownership of computing devices.
6. Competition and antitrust.
7. Software licensing and technological protection measures.
8. Hardware documentation.
9. Independent drivers and interfaces.
10. Security boundaries and legitimate proprietary protections.
11. Safety-critical systems.
12. Education, technical literacy, and experimentation.
13. Existing commercial open-hardware markets.
14. Existing government policy supporting openness.
15. Whether an "open product" can be defined in a legally workable way.

## Important distinction: open source vs. open market

"Open source" and "open market" should not automatically be treated as synonyms.

A product might contain proprietary components while still providing enough legal and technical openness for meaningful independent development.

Conversely, a product could advertise itself as open while withholding the interfaces, drivers, bootloader access, documentation, or legal permissions necessary for genuine independent development.

The project should therefore develop a functional definition of an **open-market computing product** rather than assuming that one particular open-source license answers every question.

## Guiding question

> What does ownership mean when the thing a person buys is partly a physical object and partly a controlled computer system?

A related question is:

> If computing products increasingly prevent legitimate experimentation through technical and legal restrictions, are we unintentionally narrowing the path by which technically curious people learn how systems work?

These are research questions, not predetermined conclusions.

## Next step

Before sending policy petitions or corporate letters, conduct a comparative legal and technical study of existing open-hardware products, right-to-repair law, interoperability law, technological-protection rules, and commercial developer platforms. Then define a minimum set of characteristics that would make a product genuinely "open" for this proposed market.
