# Material Definition and Choice Pathways

## Status

- **PROPOSED:** Project-specific working definition and screening approach.
- **Not a legal definition:** This document does not replace statutory, regulatory, engineering, environmental-health, or facility-specific classifications.

## Purpose

The project needs a practical way to identify materials that may create long-term pollution, disposal, recovery, or financial burdens after installation. The concern is not limited to whether a material is technically plastic. It includes whether the material becomes difficult to separate, reuse, recycle, recover, transport, or dispose of responsibly.

## Working definition of plastic burden

For this project, **plastic burden** means the difficulty, cost, restriction, risk, or lack of an accessible pathway associated with a material that is synthetic or substantially synthetic, or that contains synthetic polymers, petrochemical-derived components, coatings, binders, adhesives, foams, laminates, or composites.

A material may have a plastic burden when one or more of the following apply:

- It cannot be accepted through ordinary local disposal or recovery pathways.
- It must be separated from soil, wood, metal, masonry, or other materials before acceptance.
- It is accepted only by specialized facilities or under special conditions.
- It carries unusually high transportation, handling, testing, or disposal costs.
- It releases hazardous or persistent pollutants during use, fire, weathering, demolition, or disposal.
- It is difficult to repair, disassemble, reuse, or recover without damaging adjacent materials.
- Its composition is undisclosed or its end-of-life pathway is unknown.
- It creates a foreseeable burden for future owners, occupants, public agencies, waste workers, or surrounding communities.

## Material identity and end-of-life pathway must be recorded separately

Every material assessment should answer two distinct questions:

1. **What is the material?** Record composition, synthetic content, additives, coatings, adhesives, binders, and composite structure.
2. **What happens at the end of use?** Record repair, reuse, take-back, recycling, recovery, disposal, facility acceptance, separation requirements, cost, transport, and environmental safeguards.

A chemically synthetic material is not automatically equivalent to every other synthetic material. Conversely, a material that is not normally called plastic may still create serious disposal, contamination, toxicity, or recovery problems.

## Facility acceptance principle

Do not assume that a landfill, transfer station, recycling facility, inert-debris facility, or soil facility will accept a material merely because it appears similar to an accepted category. Acceptance must be checked with the specific facility at the time of the proposed activity.

California sources indicate that permitted waste types and actual facility acceptance are not always identical. Facility-specific verification, including current fees, conditions, and operating requirements, is therefore part of the project workflow.

## Informed material choice pathway

The project should help a resident or project sponsor make a considered decision before a material is purchased or installed. The workflow should ask:

- What material is being proposed, and what alternatives exist?
- Is the material required by current code or approval conditions, or is it a supplier, contractor, developer, or agency preference?
- What evidence supports structural, fire, seismic, moisture, health, accessibility, and durability claims?
- What is the expected service life and maintenance burden?
- Can individual components be repaired or replaced?
- Can the assembly be disassembled without contaminating or destroying other materials?
- What is the documented end-of-life pathway in the relevant county or region?
- What are the likely costs, delays, transportation needs, and handling requirements?
- Was the resident given enough time to review the choice and obtain an independent explanation?
- Were alternatives presented in plain language, including their tradeoffs and limitations?

## Time, pressure, and meaningful choice

The project should document possible decision-pressure conditions without assuming that every contractor, supplier, developer, or public agency acts improperly. A report should distinguish among:

- **DOCUMENTED:** Supported by a law, contract, written instruction, recorded meeting, or other verifiable evidence.
- **REPORTED EXPERIENCE:** Described by a resident, worker, or participant but not independently verified.
- **INTERPRETATION:** An analysis of how timing, information, authority, or economic pressure may affect choice.
- **UNKNOWN:** Information that has not yet been obtained.

A responsible pathway should identify whether people were given adequate time, understandable information, meaningful alternatives, and a realistic opportunity to request clarification or a second opinion. Urgency and survival pressures should be treated as relevant context rather than as proof of motive.

## No automatic material preference

The project does not automatically classify all plastics as prohibited or all natural materials as safe. Material selection must consider whole-building performance and lifecycle consequences, including fire, seismic behavior, moisture, indoor air quality, toxicity, durability, repairability, local skills, embodied impacts, and end-of-life handling.

The project should apply additional scrutiny when a proposed material has an unclear composition, difficult separation requirements, limited facility acceptance, high disposal costs, or an uncertain pollution pathway.

## Evidence categories

Each assessment should label conclusions as **KNOWN, CALCULATED, TESTED, PROPOSED, SPECULATIVE,** or **UNKNOWN**. Claims about safety, toxicity, recyclability, biodegradation, code compliance, fire resistance, seismic performance, or disposal acceptance must be tied to the specific product, assembly, location, hazard condition, and current authority or facility.

## Initial implementation idea

Create a screening form that produces a plain-language material record containing:

- Material and assembly name
- Location and building/project type
- Composition and suspected synthetic content
- Installation purpose
- Required performance characteristics
- Alternatives considered
- Maintenance and repair pathway
- Disassembly and separation needs
- Current recovery/disposal contacts
- Estimated cost and logistical burden
- Evidence category and confidence
- Unresolved questions
- Decision status: **retain, repair, substitute, redesign, defer, monitor,** or **unknown**

This screening form is guidance and preparation support. It is not an engineering certification, permit approval, environmental clearance, or guarantee of facility acceptance.
