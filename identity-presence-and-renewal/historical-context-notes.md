# Historical Context Notes

This document records historical ideas raised during the conversation. It is deliberately separated from the project's established premises because the examples require historical and anthropological verification.

## Cleansing as identity maintenance

The conversation raised the idea that people have historically maintained a person's social and ritual identity through practices involving bodily washing, clothing maintenance or disposal, treatment of bodily remnants, purification, and other forms of separation.

It also raised analogous practices involving written material: correction, erasure, expungement, destruction, burning, or deliberate disposal.

The project is interested in the underlying pattern:

**something associated with a person can be cleaned, separated, destroyed, renewed, or retained according to its purpose.**

## Clothing

The conversation proposed that clothing has sometimes been discarded or burned after serving a particular purpose or after becoming associated with contamination, ritual status, or transition.

Specific historical examples must be researched before being stated as general historical facts.

## Bodily remnants

The conversation mentioned hair, scabs, pus, blood, dust, and other bodily material being separated from the body and potentially destroyed or transformed into ash in ritual contexts.

These examples are not yet verified as a single historical practice or as a universal practice. They should be researched by tradition, period, place, and source.

## Writing and records

The conversation compared physical writing with digital records. Historical practices of erasure, scraping, correction, palimpsest, expungement, destruction, and burning may provide useful analogies for digital deletion and archival practice.

Again, the analogy should not be treated as evidence that every historical society understood records in the same way.

## Why this belongs in the project

The historical question is not simply whether people "deleted" things in the past. The more useful question is how societies decided that an object, record, garment, bodily remnant, or other representation should:

- remain associated with a person;
- be cleaned;
- be separated;
- be transformed;
- be destroyed;
- be archived;
- or cease to represent the person's current condition.

That is the historical question most directly relevant to digital identity lifecycle design.

## Research needed

Future research should identify well-sourced examples from different regions and periods, distinguish ritual practice from ordinary sanitation, and avoid collapsing unrelated traditions into one universal history.
