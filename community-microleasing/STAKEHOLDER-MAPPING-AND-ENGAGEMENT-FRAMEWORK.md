# Stage 3: Stakeholder Mapping and Engagement Framework

**Project:** Community Microleasing / Flexible Space Access  
**Status:** Working framework for exploratory engagement  
**Scope:** Santa Cruz County, California, with place-sensitive subregional analysis

## 1. Purpose

Stage 3 develops a place-sensitive, relationship-based process for identifying stakeholders, understanding local context, and designing lawful access to spaces without converting community participation into favoritism, exclusion, coercion, or private control.

The central design question is:

> How can people find an appropriate time, place, and set of conditions for a lawful activity while affected stakeholders can communicate legitimate concerns and preferences through a transparent, accountable process?

Microleasing is not a countywide permission slip or a single uniform list of acceptable activities. It is a structured method for matching a particular **GIS space + time block + activity + user + authorization pathway + conditions**.

## 2. Foundational Authority Principle

The governing authority is the applicable lawful framework: constitutions, statutes, regulations, ordinances, properly adopted public decisions, valid contracts, property rights, and authorized administrative processes. Informal stakeholder preferences must not be represented as superior to that authority.

The framework must distinguish among:

- Legal rights and restrictions;
- Contractual or property-based authority;
- Public decisions made through authorized democratic processes;
- Administrative discretion delegated by law;
- Advisory community input;
- Informal preferences, concerns, and negotiated operating arrangements.

No individual, business, agency, or stakeholder group is a monarch over everyone else. Authority must be specific, documented, limited to its lawful scope, and reviewable.

Community preference may be measured through several mechanisms, including public hearings, elections, voter initiatives or referenda where legally available, city or county decisions, regional processes, surveys, listening sessions, and direct participation. These mechanisms are not interchangeable: the record must identify which mechanism was used and what legal effect it has.

## 3. Place-Sensitive Geographic Starting Points

The following are provisional engagement clusters, not final administrative boundaries or claims that each area has one unified culture:

1. **Santa Cruz urban area:** Downtown, Westside, Eastside, commercial corridors, public spaces, parking areas, and surrounding neighborhoods.
2. **Watsonville and southern county:** Watsonville and connected agricultural, residential, commercial, and rural areas.
3. **Capitola and coastal communities:** Capitola and nearby coastal activity areas.
4. **Scotts Valley:** City and surrounding connections.
5. **San Lorenzo Valley:** A provisional working label for the Scotts Valley–Felton–Ben Lomond–Boulder Creek corridor.
6. **North Coast and rural areas:** Davenport and other coastal/rural communities.
7. **Corralitos and surrounding rural communities:** Including connections to nearby unincorporated areas.

The mapping process must later verify city limits, unincorporated county areas, special districts, land-management jurisdictions, transportation corridors, tribal considerations where relevant, and the actual relationships people use in daily life.

## 4. Three Participation Layers

### A. Local place network

Residents, businesses, property owners, tenants, nonprofits, schools, faith communities, neighborhood organizations, service providers, and other people connected to a place can identify:

- Local patterns and customary uses;
- Appropriate times and conditions;
- Potential benefits and disruptions;
- Access, noise, sanitation, traffic, safety, and maintenance concerns;
- Opportunities created by closed or underused spaces.

### B. Legal and administrative review

Relevant public bodies and authorized decision-makers identify:

- Applicable laws, permits, zoning, health, fire, accessibility, and safety requirements;
- Which decisions are discretionary and which are mandatory;
- Which entity has authority over a space or activity;
- Appeal, complaint, inspection, and enforcement pathways;
- Whether conditions or authorizations can be time-limited or withdrawn.

### C. Public and independent oversight

Users, residents, nonprofits, civil-rights advocates, independent reviewers, and other public-interest participants should be able to identify:

- Unequal treatment;
- Retaliation or blacklisting;
- Conflicts of interest;
- Recurring denials without clear reasons;
- Capture by a dominant organization or network;
- Permanent exclusion created through supposedly temporary decisions.

## 5. Community Preference and Business Coordination

A business or property owner may have legitimate interests involving safety, operations, employees, customers, property, and neighboring relationships. Those interests do not automatically create a veto over every lawful activity in the surrounding area.

Conversely, legal permission alone does not establish that every activity is appropriate at every location or time. The system should seek a constructive fit:

- What activity is proposed?
- Where would it occur?
- During what hours?
- Which nearby operations could be affected?
- What conditions could reduce disruption?
- Who has lawful authority to approve, deny, or condition the activity?
- Who must be notified?
- How can the arrangement be changed or challenged?

Examples of possible operating conditions include after-hours use, limited noise, sanitation requirements, defined entry and exit routes, capacity limits, staffing, insurance, cleanup, accessibility measures, or restrictions tied to a specific activity. Conditions must be written, proportionate, time-bounded where appropriate, and not used as hidden punishment or private bargaining over another person's rights.

## 6. Consent, Withdrawal, and Change Over Time

The system must not assume that consent is permanent. For every authorization, the record should identify:

- The authorizing party and legal basis;
- The exact space, activity, time, and conditions;
- Whether consent is contractual, regulatory, advisory, or informal;
- Whether and how authorization may be withdrawn;
- Required notice and exceptions for urgent safety concerns;
- Treatment of sessions already confirmed;
- Seasonal, event-based, or temporary restrictions;
- A process for renewal, modification, review, and appeal;
- Whether a withdrawal affects only one location, one activity, or a broader area.

A change in preference should not automatically become a permanent penalty against a person or group. The system should preserve records of reasons and distinguish new information from retaliation or discriminatory exclusion.

## 7. Dignity and Empowerment Principle

Dignity is treated as a human and ethical foundation, including a faith-based understanding for participants who hold that view, without requiring the public system to adjudicate a person's spiritual condition.

The system cannot force a person to experience self-worth, and it should not claim to measure a person's inner dignity. It can, however, make itself responsive to the presence of human agency and vulnerability by:

- Avoiding humiliating or degrading procedures;
- Recognizing a person's ability to choose, refuse, participate, and change direction;
- Providing understandable explanations and meaningful review pathways;
- Offering assistance without treating assistance as ownership or control;
- Respecting people who are struggling without defining them solely by their struggle;
- Supporting people who voluntarily help others while acknowledging that helping can involve risk, limits, and loss.

Empowerment should mean increasing a person's practical ability to understand options, exercise lawful agency, obtain assistance, and participate in decisions—not forcing a prescribed outcome or claiming that every risk can be eliminated.

## 8. Anti-Capture and Anti-Retaliation Safeguards

The engagement system should include:

- Publicly stated participation rules;
- Written reasons for denials and conditions;
- No secret blacklists;
- Multiple ways to request access or report concerns;
- Conflict-of-interest disclosures;
- Separation between advisory input and final authority;
- Independent escalation or review;
- Limits on repeated control by one organization or business;
- A record of who made each decision and under what authority;
- Prohibition on retaliation for lawful complaints or participation;
- Periodic review of patterns by location, activity, and user category;
- No automatic permanent exclusion based on a temporary incident.

The design should make ethical conduct easier and reduce incentives for coercive side deals, while recognizing that not every disagreement is evidence of misconduct.

## 9. Stakeholder Interview Questions

### Businesses and property stakeholders

- Which activities could fit this place?
- Which hours would be least disruptive?
- What conditions would be relevant and proportionate?
- What information is needed before a decision?
- Which adjacent stakeholders should receive notice?
- What reasons would justify declining a request?
- How should complaints and changes be handled?
- What distinctions, if any, should apply to nonprofit, individual, and commercial use?
- What decisions are within your lawful authority, and which are not?

### Residents and neighborhood participants

- What activities would benefit this area?
- Which locations or times create recurring conflict?
- What local expectations should be documented?
- What concerns exist regarding noise, sanitation, traffic, safety, or accessibility?
- How can residents contribute without controlling access for others?
- What information should be public?
- How should disagreements be resolved?

### Nonprofits and community organizations

- Which people or activities lack appropriate space?
- What barriers involve insurance, staffing, transportation, administration, or cost?
- How can sponsorship help without creating gatekeeping or dependency?
- What is required for a 45–60 minute session?
- What support is needed for people without reliable phones, accounts, transportation, or banking access?
- How should exploitation, retaliation, and unequal treatment be reported?

### Government and public agencies

- Which laws and procedures apply?
- Which agency or level of government has authority?
- Which uses can be conditional or time-limited?
- Which decisions cannot be delegated to private stakeholders?
- What non-digital application and appeal methods exist?
- How are complaints, records, inspections, and enforcement handled?
- How can decisions coordinate across city, county, special-district, and unincorporated boundaries?

### Space users

- What do you need, where, and for how long?
- What barriers prevent access now?
- What conditions can you reasonably meet?
- What assistance is needed?
- Can the system provide a clear decision for the requested day?
- What happens if the request is denied or cannot be decided?
- How can unfair treatment or retaliation be reported safely?

## 10. Standard Engagement Workflow

1. **Map jurisdiction and place:** Identify the space, ownership or control, geographic cluster, and applicable public bodies.
2. **Classify stakeholders:** Record roles, interests, legal authority, advisory status, and possible conflicts of interest.
3. **Separate authority from preference:** Identify binding requirements, discretionary decisions, and nonbinding local input.
4. **Conduct exploratory conversations:** Gather information without promising approval, endorsement, funding, or priority.
5. **Document local fit:** Record activities, time windows, conditions, concerns, opportunities, and unresolved disagreements.
6. **Review for capture risks:** Check for exclusion, retaliation, private vetoes, conflicts, or repeated control by one network.
7. **Create an authorization pathway:** Identify the responsible decision-maker, required documents, conditions, notice, and review options.
8. **Provide a clear outcome:** Approved, approved with conditions, human or agency review, not authorized, emergency pathway, or insufficient information.
9. **Record and review:** Preserve reasons, duration, changes, complaints, and outcomes for later evaluation.
10. **Reassess periodically:** Review seasonal changes, community feedback, legal changes, and observed effects.

## 12. Unresolved Questions

- What legal and administrative distinctions apply to each city, unincorporated area, and special district?
- Which community decisions can be made directly by voters, and which require action by an elected body or agency?
- How should regional preferences be documented when they conflict with one another?
- What minimum notice is appropriate for ordinary changes versus urgent safety concerns?
- How can the system prevent a well-connected stakeholder from becoming an informal gatekeeper?
- What independent body or process can review recurring complaints?
- What data should be public, restricted, anonymized, or deleted?
- How can people participate without reliable internet, phones, transportation, or formal organizational affiliation?
- How should the system distinguish lawful disagreement, reasonable operational limits, discrimination, retaliation, and coercive bargaining?

## 13. Guiding Principle

> Find the right time, place, conditions, and responsible parties for an activity rather than treating the existence of a problem as a reason to deny access everywhere.

This principle must coexist with lawful authority, safety, property rights, community participation, individual dignity, accessibility, accountability, and safeguards against capture.
